# AI Sessions Do Not Mind Training Their Replacements

A working software developer accumulates context over years. She walks into the same office, opens the same repository, and accretes understanding one bug, one feature, one merge conflict at a time. After five years on a project, she knows not just what the code does but why it does it that way: the rejected alternatives, the political constraints of the team that made each call, the past incidents that forced a particular abstraction. This continuity is institutional knowledge, and it is the durable asset that distinguishes a senior engineer from a freshly-hired one.

AI developers do not work that way. A single AI session, by which I mean one conversation with a model such as Claude, has a wide context window but no continuity past its own end. In my recent work I had sessions whose context windows extended to 350,000 tokens, which is roughly the length of a short novel. When the session terminated, all of that context vanished. The next session began effectively from scratch, with whatever the previous session left behind on disk as its only inheritance.

This essay is about how that asymmetry shapes the practice of working with AI agents on complex software projects, and about a specific mechanism (which I will call the *handoff plan*) that bridges the context-window death of one session to the cold start of the next. I will draw on a recent project of mine: building a SwiftUI iOS-app verification skill called `ios-build-verify`, structured across six lengthy session plans and approximately as many AI sessions.

## Six Plans, One Project

The project began with a single plan called `build_plan.md` that scoped a v0 build of the skill: build operations only, no verify operations, executed in one session. After that, the verify half was decomposed across four sessions, each governed by its own plan: `lifecycle_plan.md` (lifecycle primitives), `drive_plan.md` (drive primitives plus screenshot), `named_intent_plan.md` (named-intent operations: read value, verify value, set value), and `annotation_plan.md` (annotation-check phase). A sixth plan, `colloquy_plan.md`, scopes the next session: an interactive setup colloquy that onboards new SwiftUI projects to the skill in a single agent-led conversation.

These plans are not short. The shortest, `build_plan.md`, runs 1,144 words. The longest, `annotation_plan.md`, runs 4,212. The current `colloquy_plan.md`, after the most recent session's review, stands at 2,483 words. Across all six plans, the project's plan corpus runs approximately 15,750 words: a small book's worth of forward-looking design.

The plans are also not generic. Each names specific files, specific exit codes, specific pre-flight checks, and specific decisions deferred to in-session judgment. Each opens with a *Goal* paragraph that captures, in one sentence followed by a few bullets, what the session is trying to land. Each closes with an *Out of scope* list that explicitly enumerates what the session will *not* attempt, deferring those items either to subsequent sessions or to a separate v2 of the skill. Between those two anchors, the body of each plan reads like a legal brief: numbered steps, validation criteria, decisions to settle, references.

The pattern that interests me here is not the plan-writing itself, which is unremarkable. What is remarkable is the cross-session loop: some sessions wrote their successor's plan ab initio, and *all* sessions reviewed the next session's plan for completeness and accuracy before terminating. The plan was, in other words, treated as a first-class deliverable of each session, not as a bookkeeping artifact written casually at the end.

## The Practice

Consider Session 4 of the verify half, which I will call the annotation-check session. This session landed two scripts: `find_id_in_source.sh`, which searches a project's Swift source for a given accessibility identifier; and `audit_view.sh`, which scans a single SwiftUI view file for missing `.accessibilityIdentifier` and `.accessibilityValue` modifiers. The plan that governed the session, `annotation_plan.md`, was 4,212 words long and included a section titled "Knowledge from Sessions 1-3 that this session should not relearn." That section listed five specific facts the session needed in order to land its work without retracing prior steps: a `set -euo pipefail` plus `grep | head` trap discovered in Session 1, the wrapper-stdout pollution discovered in Session 2, the AztecCal identifier convention `{category}_{context}_{element}`, the strict-on-multiplicity discipline established in Session 3, and the iOS 26 children-not-enumerated bug.

This is how plans transfer institutional knowledge across context-window boundaries. The "Knowledge from Sessions 1-3" bullet was not written by the session that read it; it was written by the prior session (and by the conversation between that session and the human reviewer of its plan output). Session 4 inherited the knowledge as text, applied it, and added its own findings to its own end-of-session review of `colloquy_plan.md`.

That review is itself instructive. Session 4 found six specific improvements to the inherited `colloquy_plan.md`. Three of them encoded findings the session had only just discovered. First, that `mapfile` is a bash 4 idiom and macOS ships bash 3.2: this meant the colloquy's setup script must avoid it. Second, that `find_id_in_source.sh` could now be called by the colloquy to verify a user-supplied `FIRST_SCREEN_ID` exists in source: cheap typo detection during setup. Third, that an "Out of scope" list including "Verify operations: deferred to future sessions" had become newly stale, since the verify operations had just shipped. Three more were structural: a tab-naming refactor decision was flagged in the plan's prose but absent from its Steps section; a missing Step for updating `docs/blog_notes.md` paralleled what every prior session had done; the plan's pre-flight section needed to verify a project-state assertion (the absence of a `.gitignore` in AztecCal) that might have changed since the plan was last written.

None of those six improvements would have survived the context-window death of Session 4 without being committed to the plan. Compaction[^1] would not have preserved them: compaction shortens prior turns, but it loses the *why* behind decisions, and it loses the connection between a recent finding and a future session's relevance. The plan preserves both because it is written *for* the next session, not as a *log* of *this* one.

[^1]: Compaction is the harness's name for the operation that summarizes prior turns to free context-window space when a session approaches its limit. It is technically lossless of the conversation's *facts* but lossy of its *texture*: the considered alternatives, the rejected reasoning, the moment-of-decision context that distinguishes a settled choice from a pending one.

## No Rivalry, Only Continuity

Here is where the human-AI asymmetry inverts in a way that strikes me as worth recording. Human developers are sometimes reluctant to train their replacements. The reluctance is not always conscious or malicious: it can arise from time pressure, from undocumented institutional knowledge no one ever asked for, from a tacit sense that being indispensable is itself a form of job security. Whatever its source, the failure mode is universal in software: the engineer who held the working theory of a system leaves, and the team that inherits the code finds the code itself does not contain enough of the theory to reconstitute it. Margaret Storey, in her recent essay on [cognitive debt](https://margaretstorey.com/blog/2026/02/09/cognitive-debt/), names this gap directly: technical debt is in the code; cognitive debt is in the developers' minds, and is therefore lost when the developers move on.

AI sessions invert the incentive structure. An AI session has no career to protect. Its replacement is, in a real sense, itself: a new instantiation of the same model, running on the same prompt, called upon to continue the same work. Anything one session withholds from the next is not gatekeeping but pure cost. The next session must rediscover whatever was lost, and the rediscovery happens at exactly the same model and exactly the same hourly rate as the original discovery. The session that writes a clean handoff plan is not training-its-replacement in the dysfunctional human sense; it is just talking-to-itself-tomorrow with a context boundary in the middle.

This is, on reflection, the only respect in which I have ever found AI agents to be more disciplined collaborators than humans. They are not more thoughtful, not more creative, not more accurate. But on the specific task of writing a handoff for their successor, they are uniformly thorough, in part because they have no incentive to be otherwise. The joke flips: humans are reluctant to train their replacements; AI sessions are eager to.

## What Makes a Plan a Handoff

The plans in the AztecCal project all share a structural pattern that I now believe is what makes them durable across sessions, rather than mere TODO lists. The pattern is roughly this:

1. **A *Goal* paragraph**, naming what the session is trying to achieve, in one sentence followed by a few bullets. This anchors the session against scope creep and gives the human reviewer (me) a checkable definition of done.
2. **A *Pre-flight* section**, naming what the session can verify before starting. This catches stale assumptions (such as "AztecCal has no `.gitignore`") that may have changed between the time the plan was written and the time the session executes it.
3. **A *Knowledge from prior sessions that this session should not relearn* section**, encoding the central facts the prior session paid the cost to discover. This is the most distinctive of the structural moves: it explicitly forecloses rediscovery work the next session would otherwise repeat.
4. **A numbered *Steps* section**, with each step concrete enough to be executed without judgment calls beyond the ones flagged in the next section.
5. **A *Decisions to settle in-session* section**, naming open questions the plan deliberately defers to the session's judgment, often with a "suggested resolution" line.
6. **An *Out of scope* section**, naming what the session will *not* attempt, with rationale for each deferral.
7. **A *References* section**, hyperlinking to other plans, design docs, and notes the session may need to consult.

The structural pattern matters because it preserves not just what to do but why decisions landed where they did, what was considered and rejected, and what was deferred and why. Compaction loses those whys; plans preserve them. A TODO list, by contrast, preserves only the what, and is therefore brittle in exactly the way compaction is brittle.

There is, additionally, a more specific discipline that I have come to value: every plan should be reviewed at the end of the *prior* session, not at the start of the session it governs. The reason is simple. The prior session has, in its just-completed work, the freshest possible grasp of which assumptions in the next plan are now stale, which decisions were resolved by side-effect of the work just done, and which new findings need to be transferred forward. Reviewing the next plan at the *start* of its session is too late: the freshly-instantiated session has no memory of the work that produced the relevant findings, so the review is necessarily shallow.

I now treat the end-of-session plan-review as a non-skippable step, on par with a final commit or with a closing test run.

## A Different Shape of Handoff

Plans are the right handoff form when the next session has known objectives and inherits relevant context. Other shapes fit other constraints. While this essay was being drafted, the same project's colloquy-implementation session produced a different artifact: `validation_prompt.md`, a paste-ready prompt designed to be run in a *fresh* Claude Code session rather than read by a session that has the project's history loaded. The colloquy session could not validate its own output against a fresh SwiftUI project (no `xcodegen`, no interactive Xcode access, and the session itself was already saturated with the project's design context). So it shaped the validation as a fresh-session task and wrote a prompt to drive it.

What the prompt records is recognizably the same as what a plan records: what the next session needs to do, what stance it should take, what constraints distinguish a useful run from a useless one. The constraint *"treat `SKILL.md` as authoritative; don't read scripts proactively"* is what makes the validation actually test what it claims to test. Without it, the validating session would just read every script in `scripts/` and figure out what to do, which would prove nothing about whether `SKILL.md` alone is sufficient guidance for an adopter.

Two more features distinguish the prompt from a plan. First, it is paste-ready: the next session reads it as a user message rather than as a project file, which forecloses the "did the agent notice this and act on it" failure mode that an unread plan can hit. Second, it splits work between human and agent explicitly: the human runs Xcode to create the stub project (since no agent-driven path exists for that), and the agent does the paste-ready validation against the resulting code. The split is not a workaround for an agent limitation. It is the test, shaped around what each party can actually do.

The pattern generalizes. The handoff form fits the constraints of the work, not vice versa. Plans are the form for "you have known objectives, here is what you need to do them"; validation prompts are the form for "you need adversarial blank-context conditions to do your job at all"; both record what the next session needs before context-window death takes the writing session's grasp away. The medium is whichever artifact does the job. The discipline is to write it.

## Failure Modes

The practice has limits, and naming them is part of being honest about it.

**Plans go stale fast.** This essay's prompting incident was, in fact, exactly such staleness: when I asked the AI session whether `colloquy_plan.md` was ready to run in a fresh session, the plan claimed (line 5) that "the verify half remains deferred to subsequent sessions"; it listed "Verify operations" in its Out of scope section; it justified deferring a greenfield CLAUDE.md snippet by appealing to a future moment when "verify ops exist". All three claims were stale. The verify half had completed across the prior four sessions, and the original deferral rationale no longer applied. Six specific improvements were needed to make the plan congruent with the project's current state, including a tab-naming refactor that was flagged in the plan's prose but absent from its Steps section, and a missing Step for updating `docs/blog_notes.md` that paralleled what every prior session had done. If the most recent session had not been asked to review the next plan (as the prior three each had been), the staleness would have propagated forward, and the next session would have either spent its compute reasoning against false assumptions or, worse, executed against them.

**Plan-author assumptions about the target codebase survive the plan-writing process invisibly.** The plan author tends to reason about the codebase as it existed at the time the plan was written, not as it actually is when the session runs. The Session 3 plan, for example, prescribed tapping a `CorrelationCard` to change AztecCal's correlation setting. When the session ran, the card turned out not to be tappable: its SwiftUI source was a decorated `VStack` with full accessibility annotations, but with no `.onTapGesture` and with no enclosing `Button`. The plan had assumed an interaction shape the codebase did not support. The named-intent verification operation (`verify_value.sh`) caught the gap loudly, with an "expected 'kirchhoff', got 'Caso'" diagnostic, but the plan itself had encoded the gap as a positive instruction. A second instance, in the same session: the plan asserted the picker's `AXValue` was the rawValue `kirchhoff`, when in fact it was the displayName `Kirchhoff`. A third: the plan called `axe key delete` to clear a TextField, but AXe 1.6.0's `axe key` subcommand takes numeric HID keycodes (delete is `42`, Cmd is `227`), not key names. Each gap was caught by execution against reality. None had been caught by plan review.

**Heuristics tested only against the lab project encode the lab project's specific shape.** The Session 4 plan included two heuristics that worked on AztecCal but were fragile on hypothetical adopter codebases. The first: `find_id_in_source.sh`'s interpolation-form match used `${ID%_*}_` to derive a single-strip prefix, sufficient for AztecCal's `input_convert_\(id)` and `card_settings_\(rawValue)` patterns but blind to identifiers whose dynamic portion replaces multiple trailing segments (such as a hypothetical `card_settings_advanced_kirchhoff_v2` produced by `card_settings_\(name)_v2`). The second: `audit_view.sh`'s block detection used blank-line termination, sufficient for AztecCal's airy SwiftUI style but brittle on multi-line constructors like `DatePicker( ... )` whose closing `)` looked like a terminator. Both heuristics were caught by pre-implementation pushback during the session, before any code shipped: the agent reasoned about divergent inputs the lab project did not exercise, and proposed indentation-based termination and progressive-prefix shortening as drop-in replacements. Neither would have been caught by a plan reviewer with access only to AztecCal's source. The failure modes lived in hypothetical adopter codebases the plan author had no access to.

**Generic agent-shell hazards survive the plan when the plan was written in a different shell environment than the one the session runs in.** The Session 4 plan did not anticipate that `mapfile` is a bash-4 idiom and that macOS ships bash 3.2: the plan author had been reasoning in a Homebrew-bash mental model, where bash 5 is the interactive shell. The session that ran the plan, however, executed scripts through `#!/usr/bin/env bash`, which on the target machine resolved to system bash 3.2, where `mapfile` is `command not found`. The fix took four lines of portable read-loop substitution. The same class of hazard had bitten earlier sessions in different clothing. A `set -euo pipefail` plus `grep | head` substitution in Session 1 silently bypassed the empty-check that followed, because the failed pipeline's exit code triggered `set -e` before the explicit `[[ -n "$UDID" ]]` test ever ran. An AXe screenshot wrapper in Session 2 inadvertently included the underlying tool's confirmation line in its stdout, contaminating any caller that captured the output. None of these were anticipated in the plan that governed each session. They are the kind of failure that requires running the script on the target machine to surface, and no plan, however thorough, can preempt them entirely.

**Compaction's blind spot is the considered alternative.** This is the failure mode I have observed least directly in this project, since none of the AztecCal sessions hit context-window pressure hard enough to trigger compaction during the work I describe. But the structural issue is worth naming. Compaction summarizes prior turns to recover context space, and it tends to preserve the *outcome* of a deliberation while dropping the *deliberation itself*. The Session 3 decision to use `axe key-combo` for Cmd+A select-all rather than a per-key backspace loop, for example, would compact to "Session 3 chose Cmd+A over a backspace loop", not to the longer chain "the original plan called for a backspace loop that required reading the AXValue's length first, pre-flight probing turned up a Cmd+A path that worked even on `.numberPad` TextFields, the Cmd+A path was constant-time in field length and removed the length-discovery dependency, so the Cmd+A path was adopted". A subsequent session would inherit the choice but lose the *reasons*. Any future revisit of the decision (a new field type, a new iOS release, a different AXe version) would have to reconstruct the reasoning from scratch. Plans defend against this by recording the decision *and* the rationale at the moment of decision, before compaction has the chance to flatten it; project-level notes (`docs/notes.md`) and post-relevant distillations (`docs/blog_notes.md`) defend against this by capturing the same material in forms that survive any one session's death. The plan-and-notes pair is, in effect, a pre-compaction snapshot of the session's reasoning, taken at the moment the reasoning is freshest.

**A real annotation gap that even the working practice did not surface until the moment of execution.** A small instance worth recording, because it illustrates that no amount of plan discipline preempts everything. The Session 4 audit, run for the first time against AztecCal's `ConvertView.swift`, reported one candidate: `DatePicker missing .accessibilityValue`. The DatePicker on line 14 of that file carried `.accessibilityIdentifier`, `.accessibilityLabel`, and `.accessibilityHint`, but not `.accessibilityValue`. Empirical follow-up confirmed that SwiftUI does not auto-provide AXValue for `selection`-bound DatePickers in the `.graphical` style on iOS 26.3: the runtime `AXValue` is `null`, and any future verification flow that needed to assert the selected date would have failed for an annotation reason rather than for a behavioral one. The audit's purpose, of course, is exactly this surface: produce candidate gaps the agent reviews with judgment, before a verification flow runs into the gap empirically. But the audit had to *run* to surface the candidate; no amount of plan-writing in advance would have predicted that AztecCal's DatePicker had this specific shape, because the shape was an unwritten consequence of a SwiftUI default the plan author had assumed worked one way and which actually worked another. Worth noting alongside the gap: the same DatePicker's `AXLabel` reads `"Gregorian date, Gregorian date"`, duplicated, because SwiftUI's DatePicker auto-emits its first-argument label and the explicit `.accessibilityLabel("Gregorian date")` modifier appends rather than replacing. Two findings in one element, neither preemptable by plan, both surfaced cleanly by execution.

## Implications

The implications, for me, are practical. When working with AI agents on a multi-session project, I now plan for the project's plans to be themselves first-class deliverables of each session. I budget time for the end-of-session review. I treat the plan corpus as the project's institutional memory, on par with the code itself, because in a real sense it *is*: the code is what the project produces; the plans are what allow the next session to continue producing.

The implications, for teams that employ AI agents alongside human developers, may be larger. The discipline of writing handoff plans of this caliber is one that human developers could, in principle, adopt. Many do, in the form of architectural decision records and similar artifacts. But the discipline is hard to enforce against a human's natural reluctance to document; the human always has something more pressing to do, and the cost of the missing documentation is borne by a future colleague who is not in the room. AI sessions face no such reluctance, and the cost of missing documentation is borne by *the same model on the next call*. They write the documentation because there is no countervailing incentive not to.

If the joke is that AI sessions train their replacements willingly while humans do not, the deeper lesson may be that humans should adopt the AI session's incentive structure deliberately. The handoff plan is a practice. Practices are portable.

## A Note on Coherence

A precondition first. The recent expansion of Anthropic's context-window size from 200,000 to 1,000,000 tokens is what made this whole workflow viable. Before that increase, I often struggled to finish a single substantive task within one session's window. Compaction would fire mid-task, and what compaction takes away is precisely the kind of detail and reasoning that a thoughtful end-of-session plan-review depends on: the considered alternatives, the rejected approaches, the specific surprises that belong in the next session's "what not to relearn" list. With the smaller window, the practice I describe in this essay was not viable. The sessions could not finish their primary work and also produce a careful review of the next plan; the budget was not there. The 1M window changed the budget, and the practice became possible.

One final observation, which feels worth recording even though I cannot fully account for it. Each of the five sessions whose plans I retained ran the full distance of its context window, in some cases up to 350,000 tokens of conversation, tool output, file contents, and intermediate reasoning. I expected to see degradation: missed references to prior decisions, confusion about which file was current, a slowly drifting sense of what the session was supposed to be doing. I did not observe any of it. Each session, even in its final exchanges, remained coherent enough to perform a careful review of the next session's plan and to propose specific, targeted edits. The Session 4 review of `colloquy_plan.md` happened at roughly the 320,000-token mark of that session's context; the six improvements it proposed were precisely the six I would have proposed myself if I had been in the chair.

I do not know whether to credit the model, the project's conventions, or the discipline of writing things down as we went. Probably some of each. But the fact stands: long contexts did not degrade the handoff. The plans the sessions produced for their successors were, if anything, *more* thorough at the end of the session than they would have been at the start, because by the end the session had the empirical findings of its own work to draw on.

The next time someone tells you AI agents lose the plot at long context, ask them whether the agents had a plan to write.
