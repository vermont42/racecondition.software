# European Orthography: Conversation Notes and Book Plan

Captured from a conversation on 2026-04-19 while working on AztecCal. The conversation drifted from a stray comment about the English spelling of "separation" into a multi-hour tour of European orthographic development, reform, and politics. This document preserves the substantive material, organized for future use as research toward a potential retirement book.

## Core Synthesis: The Frontier / Interior / Purge Typology

The most useful framework that emerged. Orthographic and lexical reform operates under three distinct regimes, each with its own conditions for success:

- **Frontier regime.** Protection works here. Institutions can shape which new loans enter the language. Example: Quebec's OQLF resisting new English loans into Quebec French.
- **Interior regime.** Reform and purge both fail here. Words embedded deeply enough are effectively immovable. Examples: Romanian *da* surviving the 19th-century Latinist purge; Quebec *fun* surviving Bill 101.
- **Purge regime.** Active removal of existing loans works only when the target is both **recent** and **politically charged**. Examples: post-1989 Romanian de-russification (*soviet*, *kolhoz*, *politburo* dropped quickly); Atatürk's 1928 Turkish script and vocabulary reforms.

**Implication for policy**: a protectionist agency should triage explicitly by regime rather than treating all borrowings as a single class. Agencies that do this implicitly (OQLF) survive and are effective. Agencies that attempt to fight the interior (the extremist fringe of any purification movement) lose credibility and fail. Agencies that intervene only at the frontier but don't describe their logic get mischaracterized.

## Case Studies by Pattern

### Nationalist Archaization

- **Greek Katharevousa.** Adamantios Korais (1748–1833), working in Enlightenment Paris, proposed Katharevousa (literally "purifying") as a reach backward to Classical and Koine Greek, explicitly political — restoring linguistic independence Ottoman rule had eroded. Reinforced by Western European philhellenism (Byron, Shelley) framing Greeks as "heirs of Homer" and by the Megali Idea needing a continuity narrative from antiquity through Byzantium to the modern state. Demotic wins only in 1976, once the Ottoman trauma had cooled enough for archaizing performance to stop being necessary.
- **Faroese Hammershaimb orthography (1846).** The clearest documented blank-slate case. V.U. Hammershaimb explicitly chose Old Norse etymology over Jens Christian Svabo's earlier phonetic-orthography proposal — specifically so that written Faroese would look Icelandic and assert Norse heritage against Danish cultural subordination. Modern result: spelling opacity comparable to Danish, but by conscious choice rather than drift.
- **Hebrew revival.** Ideology (Ben-Yehuda's Sephardic pronunciation target) lost to phonology (adult Ashkenazi pioneers couldn't retrain their uvular R). The first native-Israeli-Hebrew generation acquired what their parents physically produced. Uvular R in Modern Hebrew is the single major phonological feature where Ashkenazi won over the explicit Sephardic target.
- **Turkish language reform (1928).** Atatürk's Arabic-to-Latin script change plus aggressive purge of Arabic and Persian loanwords toward an imagined pre-Islamic Turkic origin. The most successful authoritarian-imposed reform in the modern era.
- **Finnish nationalism (19th century).** Folk-vernacular revival, Kalevala-era vocabulary, distinguishing from Swedish dominance.
- **Catalan Renaixença.** Medieval Catalan revived against Castilian Spanish.

### Latinist Purification

- **Romanian, 19th century.** The Transylvanian School (*Școala Ardeleană*) — Samuil Micu, Gheorghe Șincai, Petru Maior, Greek Catholic clerics trained in Rome and Vienna — argued the Daco-Roman continuity theory: Romanians as descendants of Roman colonists submerged under Slavic contact. Cyrillic replaced by Latin alphabet (1862). Slavic vocabulary replaced with Latin and French borrowings. But deep vocabulary survived: *da, prieten* (friend), *nevastă* (wife), *trebuie* (must), *iubi* (to love) — all Slavic, all too embedded to dislodge. Laurian's extreme Latinist dictionary (1871–76) so aggressive it was unreadable; Maiorescu's Junimea society rejected the extreme and argued for moderation. The purge succeeded in moderated form — Latinized surface, Slavic-colored intimate register — leaving modern Romanian at ~30% Slavic vocabulary, down from an estimated pre-reform 50%.
- **Italian's pre-modern Italianization of Latinate spellings.** *Philosophia* → *filosofia*, *theoria* → *teoria*, Latin *y* → *i*, "ph" and "th" digraphs dropped. This happened during the late Medieval and Renaissance periods, centuries before unification. Compare French, which kept *philosophie* and *théorie*. Same Romance ancestor, opposite orthographic choice, driven by Dante/Petrarch/Boccaccio's vernacular-based literary tradition.

### Failed or Partial Reform

- **Danish.** The 1948 reform removed noun-capitalization (shedding a German convention) and changed *aa* → *å*. But Danish pronunciation has drifted dramatically since the 17th century — consonant lenition (intervocalic /b, d, g/ → fricatives or silent), vowel reduction, /r/ as a pharyngeal vowel-colorer, unwritten stød — without orthographic follow-through. Reasons for the gap: literary continuity with Kierkegaard, Andersen, Holberg; cross-Scandinavian written legibility with Norwegian Bokmål and Swedish; elite comfort with the current system; no political crisis forcing reform.
- **Norwegian Bokmål (contrast).** Did reform aggressively because Norway needed to distinguish from Danish after 1814 independence. Knud Knudsen from the 1850s onward, continuing through 1917, 1938, and 1981 revisions. Denmark lacked the equivalent political motive. Norwegian Bokmål is what Danish spelling would look like today if Denmark had needed one.
- **French 1990 rectifications orthographiques.** Modest — circumflex accents removed from most *i* and *u*, some hyphenation adjusted, plural of compounds regularized. Submitted as recommendations, not rules. Widely ignored by publishers, schools, and users. Demonstrates that a centralized authority without enforcement power can't push even modest reform through.
- **Greek 1982 monotonic.** Replaced the ancient polytonic accent system with a single accent plus diaeresis. Fiercely contested at the time, now universal. But the /i/ spelling question (six distinct orthographic representations: η, ι, υ, ει, οι, υι, all pronounced /i/) was not touched. Accents were politically tractable; deep vowel-spelling reform was not.

### Successful Inheritance Rather Than Reform

- **Italian.** The 19th-century unification *ratified* a convergence that had been accumulating for 500 years through the Florentine literary tradition. Dante's *De vulgari eloquentia* (c. 1305) had already argued for vernacular. Manzoni's *I promessi sposi* (1827, revised 1840) cemented modern Florentine as the basis. Italian's relatively transparent orthography is a side effect of this accident — the literary prestige language was vernacular, so there was no Latin-etymologizing authority to preserve archaic spellings.
- **Spanish.** Continuous regularization by the Real Academia Española since 1815. Multiple small reforms gradually bringing spelling into closer alignment with pronunciation. Works because the central authority is genuinely active.
- **Swedish 1906.** Removed silent *h* (*hvad* → *vad*, *hvem* → *vem*), regularized vowel doubling. Moderate scope, accepted by publishers and schools, successful. Contrast with Danish, which did not go through an equivalent reform.

### Blank-Slate Design

- **Faroese (Hammershaimb 1846)** stands alone as a case where orthographic choice was made deliberately, against a named alternative (Svabo's phonetic proposal), with full knowledge of the trade-off. Most other "designed" orthographies (Turkish, Korean Hangul, various colonial alphabets) were imposed top-down; Faroese was chosen by the linguistic community from competing proposals.
- **Glagolitic (Cyril, c. 863 CE).** A second blank-slate case worth pairing with Faroese. Cyril designed Glagolitic for the Moravian mission to give Old Church Slavonic a written form. Not Greek-derived despite Cyril's Byzantine Greek background — the letterforms are distinctive and rounded, possibly drawing on Samaritan, Coptic, and Hebrew models. Unlike Faroese, Glagolitic did not survive in broad use: after Cyril's and Methodius's deaths, disciples in Bulgaria created a Greek-uncial-based replacement (what became Cyrillic) and named it after Cyril, even though the new alphabet was not his design. Glagolitic persisted only in Croatian Catholic liturgy into the 20th century. See also the "Fame Tracks Transmission" section below.

### Identity-Marker Orthographies: The Balkans as Concentrated Lab

In a small geographic area speaking variants of one language — **BCMS** (Bosnian, Croatian, Montenegrin, Serbian), all essentially Shtokavian — three scripts have operated simultaneously, each tracking religious and political identity rather than any structural linguistic feature:

- **Latin alphabet** — Croatian (Catholic, Habsburg and Venetian orbit); Bosnian and Montenegrin (more recent adoption).
- **Cyrillic alphabet** — Serbian (Orthodox, Byzantine and Ottoman orbit, refined by Vuk Karadžić's 19th-century phonetic reform: "write as you speak, read as written"); Montenegrin officially accepts both scripts.
- **Arabic alphabet (Arebica)** — historically Bosnian Muslim, before settling on Latin in modern times.

Same language, four slight standard variants, three active historical scripts, driven almost entirely by religious-political identity. Karadžić's Cyrillic reform is a nice secondary point: making the script more phonetic and internally simple *strengthened* its identity function, because it made Serbian visually and structurally distinctive from Croatian Latin even though the spoken language was the same.

The Croatian **Glagolitic tradition** is a unique case within this picture — a Catholic Slavic-liturgical script (neither Cyrillic-derived nor Latin-derived) that served as a bridge between Latin-Catholic identity and Slavic-speaking communities on the Dalmatian coast. The last Glagolitic missal was published in 1893; the script persisted in some Croatian Catholic churches into the 20th century. When Glagolitic waned, the Catholic framework made Latin the natural successor rather than Cyrillic — **religious affiliation overriding any structural similarity to Serbian**.

**Why this is the cleanest case for the "orthography as identity marker" claim**: the linguistic substrate (BCMS) is essentially constant across all the variation. Whenever orthographic difference elsewhere in Europe correlates with a structural linguistic difference, you can argue the spelling simply tracks the language. Here there's no such confound — one language, three scripts, every bit of variation driven by religion and political alignment. The Balkans are the cleanest demonstration in Europe of orthography functioning as pure identity marker. Every other case study has at least some linguistic cover for the choice; the Balkan case strips that cover away.

## Recurring Patterns and Principles

### Orthography as Multi-Purpose Cultural Artifact

Any orthographic system is simultaneously doing: phonetic representation, literary-continuity preservation, political-identity marking, morphological disambiguation, prestige signaling, cross-community legibility. Reducing the analysis to a single frame (usually "does the spelling match the sound?") misses what's actually happening. The anthropological reading applied to writing systems is the most productive analytical move.

### Demographics Determine Standard (the Hebrew Principle)

Who produces the first generation of native speakers sets the phonology, regardless of stated ideological preference. Ben-Yehuda himself — the ideologue of Sephardic pronunciation — almost certainly used uvular R because he was a native Lithuanian Yiddish speaker. The revival locked in what the founding demographic could physically produce, not what they prescribed. Counterfactual: had early Zionism's demographic center been New York rather than Odessa, Modern Hebrew might well use English approximant R.

### Ease of Articulation Drives Phonological Change

The Great R Shift across Europe (17th–19th century): alveolar trill → uvular in French, German, Portuguese, Dutch, Swedish. The trill is intrinsically unstable — hard to learn, hard to produce reliably even for natives. Spanish-speaking children show persistent *rotacismo*, typically acquiring the trill only at ages 4–6, sometimes later. Hebrew's Ashkenazi pioneers couldn't retrain this consonant as adults. The phoneme is genuinely harder than most, and languages drift away from it when the prestige conditions permit.

### Orthography Preserves Information Phonology Has Lost

- Greek /i/ spellings encode grammatical gender/number/person (masculine plural *-οι*, feminine singular *-η*, verb endings *-ει/-εις*).
- Danish silent consonants preserve morphological distinctions the pronunciation has erased.
- French silent verb endings (*-s, -nt*) carry person/number information — though **redundantly**, since French is strict non-pro-drop and the pronoun already carries it. The functional argument for conservative spelling is strongest where the phonology has lost information, weakest where the redundancy is complete (French).

### Etymology as Spelling Heuristic

The French-pronunciation trick for English spelling: *séparation* with its clear /a/ disambiguates *separation* vs. *seperation*. Works for roughly 60% of English vocabulary that comes from Latin or French (counting French intermediaries). Doesn't work for the Germanic half, which spells more phonetically anyway and doesn't benefit from etymological backtracking.

Related etymology-based tools:
- *Definite* (Latin *definitus*) — no 'a'.
- *Privilege* (Latin *privilegium*) — no 'd'.
- *Accommodate* (Latin *ad-* + *commodus*) — double *c*, double *m*, both from the prefix-plus-stem analysis.
- *Occurrence* — double *r* from the *currere* (running) stem.

### The -dge / -ge Pattern

Not purely etymological but emerged from Germanic palatalization patterns. Modern rule:
- Short vowel + /dʒ/ in stressed syllable → **-dge** (*bridge, ledge, judge, lodge*).
- Long vowel / diphthong / consonant + /dʒ/ → **-ge** (*cage, huge, large, hinge*).
- Latinate multi-syllable → **-ge** (*privilege, college, prestige*).

*Judge* is Romance-origin (Old French *juge*, Latin *judex*) but spelled with the Germanic -dge pattern because the vowel is short. The convention's origin was Germanic; its generalization is phonological. Useful disambiguation between two rules needed for English spelling: the etymology heuristic for Latinate multi-syllables, the vowel-length rule for monosyllables.

### English Preserves Medieval French Sounds

The /dʒ/ in *judge* and the /tʃ/ in *church* are sounds Old French had and modern French no longer has. English borrowed them during the Norman and post-Norman period; French then simplified /dʒ/ → /ʒ/ and /tʃ/ → /ʃ/ between the 13th and 16th centuries. Modern English is thus phonologically closer to medieval French than modern French is, at least for these specific affricates.

French is currently **re-importing /dʒ/** from two directions:
- **English loans**: *jazz, jogging, jean, job, DJ* — spelled with native English *j* but pronounced /dʒ/. Accepted spelling/sound mismatch.
- **Arabic loans**: *djinn, djihad, djebel, Djibouti, hadj* — spelled with explicit *dj* digraph to flag the non-native pronunciation.

French orthography has acquired dual conventions for /dʒ/, asymmetrically applied: *j* for English loans, *dj* for Arabic/Slavic loans. This is the context the *cràcher* proposal addresses.

### The Cràcher Proposal

From racecondition.software/blog/spelling/: proposes French *crasher* (to crash) → *cràcher*. Two moves:
1. Replace *sh* (not native French) with *ch* (native French representation of /ʃ/).
2. Mark the *a* with grave accent to disambiguate from *cracher* (to spit) — pure orthographic disambiguation, no phonetic work.

Demonstrates that:
- French orthography has the tools for systematic loan nativization (grave accent as disambiguator, *ch* digraph, *dj* for /dʒ/).
- The Académie française has the authority to act but has chosen not to.
- The historical precedent — *boulingrin* (bowling green), *redingote* (riding coat), *paletot* (Middle English *paltock*), *bifteck* (beefsteak), *poudingue* (pudding) — demonstrates that the convention used to be productive and simply stopped.
- English prestige is the force that stopped it. A 17th-century English loan carried no cultural charge; re-spelling it was natural. A modern loan carries signals of modernity and international chic; re-spelling it neuters the signal.

The proposal is a real reform suggestion, not a joke, and it foregrounds that the Académie's passivity is a choice rather than a necessity.

### The Anglosphere "Protective France" Myth

The popular narrative conflates three distinct things:
- **Académie française**: no enforcement power, forty elderly Immortals, glacial dictionary updates, mostly moral authority.
- **Toubon Law (1994)**: requires French in public/commercial contexts; doesn't give the Académie enforcement teeth.
- **Quebec's OQLF**: the actually-aggressive francophone institution, with legal teeth under Bill 101, compliance officers, fines, active terminology creation, and post-*fun* triage of frontier vs. interior cases.

Real francophone linguistic enforcement happens in Montreal, not Paris. The "protective France" narrative is usually describing Quebec while crediting France.

**The narrative as motte-and-bailey** (Nicholas Shackel, 2005). This myth fits Shackel's motte-and-bailey pattern cleanly, and naming the pattern gives the analysis a sharper spine:

- **Bailey (the attractive claim)**: France aggressively polices its language; the Académie française actively enforces French purity; French authorities restrict anglicisms through legal and cultural mechanisms.
- **Motte (the defensible retreat)**: France has a language academy that publishes guidance, and the Toubon Law requires French in some public contexts.

The motte is true. The bailey is a mischaracterization that slides from "these institutions exist" to "these institutions function as the narrative portrays." When challenged on specifics — *can* the Académie actually enforce anything? *does* the Toubon Law mandate orthographic reform? — defenders retreat to the motte, and the bailey quietly reasserts itself the next time the topic comes up. The chapter on this myth should make the slide explicit; naming the pattern also gives readers a tool for spotting similar moves in other linguistic-protectionism narratives (German Duden authority, Icelandic purism, the Turkish Language Association, the RAE, etc.). Motte-and-bailey is a useful enough analytical lens to deploy recurrently — it applies as well to the "Germanic is uniquely divergent" claim flagged in the McWhorter caveat, and to the "Aztec calendar" framing corrected in the Fame-Tracks-Transmission section. Worth introducing once, in a footnote with the Shackel citation, and then reusing as shorthand throughout.

### Fame Tracks Transmission, Not Innovation

A pattern about naming conventions, related to but distinct from the reform patterns. **The culture that becomes internationally famous for a cultural technology is often not the most sophisticated originator but the most effective consolidator at the moment of highest-visibility transmission to the eventual naming culture.** Fame attaches at the point of contact, not at the point of origination.

Case studies:

- **Arabic numerals → Indian numerals.** Decimal positional notation with zero developed in India (Aryabhata c. 499 CE, Brahmagupta c. 628 CE, *Brahmasphutasiddhanta* formalizing arithmetic with zero). Al-Khwarizmi's *On the Calculation with Hindu Numerals* (c. 820 CE) transmitted the system to the Arab world and **explicitly credits India**. Arabs have always called them *al-arqam al-hindiyya* ("Hindu numerals"). Europeans encountered them via Arab intermediaries (Al-Andalus, Sicily, Fibonacci) and named them after the intermediary rather than the originator. Al-Khwarizmi's own name gave English "algorithm" and "algebra" — so English borrowed from the transmitter in one place (numerals, misattributed) and from the transmitter's *name* in another (algorithm, algebra), in neither case naming the deeper Indian/Babylonian/Greek sources al-Khwarizmi himself drew from.
- **Cyrillic → "Clement-and-Naumic."** Cyril created the **Glagolitic** alphabet (c. 863 CE), not Cyrillic. After Cyril and Methodius died, their disciples were expelled from Great Moravia and welcomed in Bulgaria. There — likely under Clement of Ohrid and Naum, though attribution is contested — they created a new alphabet based on Greek uncial letters, with additional characters (some adapted from Glagolitic) for Slavic-specific sounds. They named this new alphabet after their teacher, even though it was not his design. Glagolitic persisted in Croatian Catholic liturgy into the 20th century; Cyrillic spread everywhere else. Cyril is famous for an alphabet he didn't create; his actual creation is a footnote. **A small honesty in the misattribution**: Cyrillic's Slavic-specific letters (ж, ш, щ, ч, ц) were adapted from Glagolitic, so Cyrillic does inherit Cyril's phonological analysis even though the visible letterforms are Greek. A more accurate English name would be "Clement-and-Naumic," honoring the actual authors. It obviously wouldn't have caught on.
- **Roman alphabet → Etruscan-via-Greek-via-Phoenician alphabet.** Rome inherited the alphabet, much of its early calendar (Etruscan month names), its pantheon (renamed Greek gods — Zeus/Jupiter, Ares/Mars, Aphrodite/Venus), the arch (Etruscan), philosophy and literature (Greek). The Roman synthesis got the fame; the inheritance chain became a footnote even for educated readers.
- **Aztec calendar → Mesoamerican calendar.** The 260-day + 365-day combination is documented at Monte Albán (Zapotec) during the Classical period, centuries before the Mexica arrived in central Mexico as late-coming nomads. The Mexica were late-arriving military consolidators who standardized and elaborated an inherited system. International fame attached to the Mexica version because the Mexica were the dominant power at the moment of Spanish contact — the culture with the high-profile collapse worth writing about.

Dozens of familiar misnomers follow the same structure: *French fries* (Belgian), *English muffin* (American invention), *Jerusalem artichoke* (North American; not from Jerusalem, not an artichoke), *Chinese checkers* (German, originally *Stern-Halma*), *Arabic coffee* (Ethiopian origin, Yemeni cultivation, Arab distribution), *Spanish flu*, *Indian summer*, *Dutch courage*. In each case the name attaches at the namer's point of first encounter, not at the point of true origin.

**Book implication**: this pattern is a recurring motif useful throughout the book rather than a standalone chapter. A chapter on the Roman alphabet can open with its Etruscan-Greek-Phoenician inheritance; a chapter on Cyrillic can correct the Cyril-did-not-make-Cyrillic misattribution early; a chapter on the "Aztec" calendar can restore the Mesoamerican context; the Arabic-numerals case is a good opening anecdote establishing the author's habit of checking who actually invented what. Correcting misnomers is a clean narrative hook in popular nonfiction — readers enjoy the "wait, really?" moment, and the correction builds author credibility for the harder reinterpretations later in the book.

## Tools the Reformer Has Available

Three distinct approaches to how orthography responds to borrowing. Each has its own success conditions:

1. **Lexical replacement.** Coin or revive a native equivalent. *Baladeur* for *Walkman*, *courriel* for *email*, *mot-dièse* for *hashtag*. Success depends on uptake, which depends on prestige. Works when the coined term has independent appeal (succinctness, native sound-feel); fails when it's obviously bureaucratic (*inforoute* for *internet*, rejected by users).
2. **Phonetic re-spelling (nativization).** Adapt the spelling of the loan to match native orthography. *Redingote, boulingrin, bifteck*. Historical in French, dormant for modern loans, proposed for revival by *cràcher*-type reforms.
3. **Accept and mark.** Keep the original spelling as a signal of foreign origin. What French does with most modern English loans now. The *dj* digraph is a compromise between this and phonetic re-spelling: explicit foreign-marker but uses native sound-to-spelling rules.

## Book Project: Advice Summary

### Motivation and Stakes

The project exists because the author wants people to **enjoy this knowledge** — to encounter the patterns and case studies that have accumulated through years of reading and conversation, and find them as interesting as he does. This is the stated North Star, and it's load-bearing for downstream decisions.

What this orientation distinguishes the project from:

- **Personal-accomplishment framing.** This is not primarily about "I want to have published a book" or "I want a career capstone." The artifact is in service of transmission, not the other way around.
- **Commercial-success-first framing.** Sales matter as a proxy for reach, not as the goal itself. The success measure is "more people encountered and enjoyed this material," not "the book grossed $X" or "the channel hit Y subscribers."
- **Academic-prestige-first framing.** The author has anthropology and French-literature training plus engineering and iOS-developer experience, not a linguistics doctorate. The project isn't optimized for tenure-track or academic-credentialing purposes; it's optimized for popular accessibility while preserving real rigor.

What this orientation implies for downstream decisions:

- **Free / accessible distribution prioritized.** YouTube over paid lecture platforms; trade-press book over academic monograph; companion-website audio resources free rather than gated.
- **Voice that conveys delight prioritized over voice that signals authority.** McWhorter's accessible enthusiasm as the closer model; Coulmas's careful academic precision as the more distant one.
- **Lifetime durability prioritized.** Books still being read in 2070 matter more than peak attention in 2036. (Aligns with the *Story of English* longevity observation — the book has outlived the broadcasts.)
- **Multiple smaller entry points prioritized over single big launches.** Shareable examples (*Lānaʻi* / *lānai*, Cook's *Owhyhee* maps, Niʻihau's /t/, Hawaiian *w*-as-[β], the Cyrillic-isn't-Cyril gotcha, Hebrew uvular R), short videos, blog posts, conference talks, podcast appearances — many routes to discovery. The book is a deep destination, but the work that brings readers to it is built from many small entry points.

The audience-finding risk is real and honest: **no project is guaranteed to find its audience.** Mitigation comes from the dual-format strategy (book + video), the nine-year audience-building runway, multiple shareable hooks built into the material, network connections with adjacent linguistics-YouTube creators that can be cultivated during the runway, the genuinely underserved orthography niche, and the author's rare cross-medium capability combination. None of these guarantee success; together they make zero-audience outcomes considerably less likely than for a less-prepared project.

Base rates in popular linguistics are reassuringly bimodal: many projects find modest-but-real audiences (5K–50K readers, 10K–100K subscribers); some break through to large audiences; very few find zero audience. The realistic risk is **"smaller audience than hoped" rather than "no audience at all"** — and a smaller-than-hoped audience that genuinely engages with the material is closer to the stated goal than a large indifferent audience would be.

**The success measure to hold onto is not "how many readers" but "did at least some readers encounter and enjoy this."** A modest audience that gets the *Lānaʻi* / *lānai* "wait, really?" pleasure is closer to the goal than a large audience that doesn't. The work is *for* that reader — not for the metrics dashboard.

Typology over chronology. Organize by regime:

### Notation and Format Decisions

Two cross-cutting design decisions that shape what the book can actually do, both worth committing to early.

#### Use IPA, not English-respelling

**Use IPA, not Wikipedia-style English respelling.** Wikipedia's pronunciation system (e.g., *kuh-WAH-ee* for *Kauaʻi*, *muh-NOM-ih-nee* for *Menominee*) works fine for English-internal pronunciation guides and would be the right call for a book whose subject is *only* English spelling. But a comparative European / cross-linguistic orthography book covers Hawaiian /β/, French nasals, Welsh /ɬ/, Greek /θ/ vs. /ð/, Spanish /ɲ/, German /x/, Mandarin tones, Finnish vowel length, and dozens of other sounds the Wikipedia respelling system can't represent without contortion or English-cognate guesswork. **IPA is the only notation that scales to the book's actual subject matter.** Once that's accepted, the question is *how to teach IPA gracefully*, not *whether to use it*.

Models for teaching IPA to a general reader:

- **Steven Pinker, *The Language Instinct*** (1994). Introduces a focused IPA subset compactly in chapter 6, with a single reference chart and consistent first-use definitions throughout. Works for a general audience. **The best model for this book's register.**
- **Geoffrey Sampson, *Writing Systems*** (1985, rev. 2015). Opens with a focused IPA-subset chart limited to the symbols actually used in the book — the "linguist's working subset" rather than full IPA. Slightly more technical than Pinker but still tractable.
- **John McWhorter, *Our Magnificent Bastard Tongue*** (2008). Uses IPA sparingly and English-style respelling more often, which works because his book is English-focused. Wouldn't scale to this book's comparative scope; informative as a contrast.
- **Florian Coulmas, *Writing Systems: An Introduction to Their Linguistic Analysis*** (2003). Full academic IPA with no general-reader concessions. Overkill for the popular-academic register this book targets but useful as a reference.

Recommended structural approach:

1. **An opening "Reading IPA" chapter** (or front-matter section referenced from chapter 1). Focused on the 40–60 symbols and conventions actually used in the book, not the full IPA chart.
2. **A per-chapter cheat sheet** at the start of each language-specific chapter showing the IPA symbols that chapter uses, with English-pronunciation glosses where they exist.
3. **First-use definitions** in chapter text — "/β/ as in Spanish *cabo*" the first time /β/ appears in each chapter, with a quick reminder rather than expecting the reader to remember from the opening.
4. **A consolidated IPA chart at the back of the book** for quick reference. Plus a glossary of recurring terms.

**Bonus selling point**: most existing popular orthography books either (a) avoid IPA and stay English-focused, sacrificing scope; or (b) use IPA but pretend audio doesn't matter, sacrificing accessibility. A book that takes IPA seriously *and* provides a multimedia companion (see below) is doing something the existing market doesn't.

#### The Sound-and-Format Problem

The single most awkward thing about a book on orthography is that **the subject is the relationship between writing and sound, but a printed book can only carry the writing**. Every contrast the book discusses — Hawaiian /w/ as [β] vs. [w], the *Lānaʻi* / *lānai* minimal pair, Mandarin tone marks, Vietnamese tone-letter combinations, French nasal vowels, Welsh *ll* — has an *audio* component the page can't render. Most popular linguistics books paper over this with approximations ("pronounced roughly like..."); the more rigorous the book wants to be, the more this papering-over becomes a structural problem.

Options for closing the gap, ranked by ease-of-execution:

- **Companion website** with audio for every IPA example and every comparative-pronunciation discussion. Low overhead, easy to update, works for both print and ebook readers. QR codes embedded next to print examples linking to specific audio clips on the site. **Probably the right first ship**: easy to set up alongside the book launch, doesn't constrain the book's primary format, and can scale up later if the audience exists.
- **Audiobook**. The strange constraint of an audiobook *for this book specifically*: it **inverts the normal medium-message relationship**, because the visual representation of writing is half the subject. The listener hears the sound but can't see the writing. Possible solutions: (a) audiobook with companion PDF showing the orthographic forms; (b) audiobook structured around verbal descriptions of writing-system features ("the *ʻokina* looks like an inverted single quotation mark sitting above the line"). The audiobook is genuinely harder than it looks and might be best released as a companion to the print book rather than a primary format.
- **Video format**. The cleanest solution to the multimodal problem: video can show writing *while* playing sound, with no inversion of medium-message relationship. Models include **John McWhorter's Great Courses lectures**, **Anne Curzan's Great Courses lectures** on English linguistics, **Daniel Jurafsky's *Language of Food*** companion website, and the **NativLang / Langfocus / Tom Scott on linguistics** YouTube traditions. A video companion series — even short YouTube episodes paralleling each chapter — would handle the sound-and-writing problem natively. Higher production overhead than a static website but far more demonstrative.
- **Multimedia ebook**. EPUB 3 and fixed-layout Apple Books / iBooks formats support embedded audio. Technically viable but limits distribution to ebook readers that handle it well, and the embedded-audio format has had a rough adoption history. Less viable as the primary format; might work as a value-added ebook tier.

**Recommended sequence**: print book as primary format, companion website with QR-coded audio as default multimedia layer at launch, audiobook and/or video as later expansion *if* the book finds a successful audience. **Address the sound issue explicitly in the introduction**, not papered over: telling the reader "this is a book about a relationship between writing and sound; the writing is on the page, and the sound lives at *url*" is honest, helpful, and sets up the chapter-by-chapter QR codes as obviously useful rather than gimmicky.

The multimedia-companion plan is also a **selling-point differentiator** against the existing market. A book pitch that says "this is the first orthography book that takes both IPA rigor *and* the actual sound of the languages seriously, with a companion audio resource so the reader can hear the contrasts" is doing something most competitors aren't.

Video alone carries both *simultaneously*, in real time, with no apologetic gap between the visual and the auditory. **If "ideal" means "matches the content's multimodal nature most directly," video is genuinely the ideal format for an orthography book** — and that observation deserves a strategic rethink rather than being absorbed into the companion-at-launch plan.

### Finnish as the Comparison Class: The Shallow-Orthography Success Story

Finnish is treated in one line under nationalist archaization. That undersells it — Finnish is actually a *different category*, the **anchor case for "successful design, sustained transparency"**, and arguably the most important contrast point in the book. The interesting elements:

- **Mikael Agricola (1543).** *ABCkiria*, the first printed Finnish book, established orthographic conventions still recognizable today. Agricola was a Lutheran reformer educated in Wittenberg, designing for Bible translation — which gave him both authority and a strong consistency incentive. Crucially, he had **no prior literary tradition to negotiate around**. Finnish was a vernacular without canonical written works, so there was no etymologizing or prestige-preserving pressure pulling against phonetic transparency.
- **The orthographic design choices.** Near-1-to-1 grapheme-phoneme correspondence. Doubled letters for long phonemes — both vowel and consonant — making length contrasts explicit: *tuli* "fire" / *tuuli* "wind" / *tulli* "customs", three different words distinguished only by which letter is doubled. Vowel harmony made visible orthographically (the a/ä, o/ö, u/y pairs preserve the agreement rule across suffixes). No silent letters, no etymological spellings, no contested digraphs.
- **Elias Lönnrot and the Kalevala (1835).** Compilation of folk poetry into a national epic. Standardized the literary register and ratified the Agricolan orthography by deploying it on a prestige-cultural canvas. This is *the* Finnish nation-building moment, and it happens without any orthographic reform agenda — because the orthography didn't need reforming.
- **The 19th-century institutional ratification.** Tsar Alexander II's 1863 language decree elevated Finnish to official status alongside Swedish. The *fennomania* movement pushed Finnish into administration, education, and literature. Throughout, no major reform was attempted because the orthography was already transparent enough to be uncontested.
- **The empirical payoff: Seymour, Aro & Erskine (2003).** Cross-language reading-acquisition study comparing first-grade readers across European orthographies. Finnish-speaking children achieved near-ceiling word-decoding accuracy within months; English-speaking peers needed 2–3 years to reach equivalent fluency. **The strongest empirical argument for orthographic reform isn't elegance — it's literacy outcomes.** Finnish provides the comparison class for what reform could theoretically achieve.

Chapter framing: Finnish is the natural counterfactual — what European orthographies *could* look like if they'd been designed afresh in the 16th century by a single reformer without a prestige literary tradition to negotiate around. The fact that no other major European language has this profile is itself the finding. The chapter pairs naturally with the Greek and Romanian chapters as a "what successful reform looks like when the conditions are favorable" comparison.

### Phonological-Depth Framework: A Theoretical Spine for the Closing Chapters

The current outline is organized around political/cultural cases. A complementary theoretical framework worth introducing is **orthographic depth** (Liberman, Frost, Katz, Seymour; developed across cognitive-linguistics literature 1980s–2010s):

- **Shallow orthographies**: near-1-to-1 grapheme-phoneme correspondence. Finnish, Spanish, Italian, Croatian (post-Vuk Cyrillic), Serbian Cyrillic, Vietnamese.
- **Deep orthographies**: phoneme-to-grapheme mapping is many-to-many; orthography preserves morphological, etymological, and historical information at the cost of decoding transparency. English, French, Modern Greek (the /i/ problem), Tibetan.

This isn't just typological taxonomy — it has empirical consequences:

- **Reading acquisition speed** is faster in shallow orthographies (Seymour et al. 2003; Ziegler & Goswami 2005).
- **Dyslexia** presents differently across orthographies — phonological-processing deficits are more debilitating in deep orthographies because the reader can't fall back on direct grapheme-phoneme decoding. The dyslexia diagnosis rate varies meaningfully by orthographic depth even when underlying neurology is constant.
- **Adult reading speed converges** across orthographies — skilled readers of deep orthographies develop whole-word recognition that compensates. The acquisition cost is front-loaded; steady-state competent reading is comparable.
- **Morphological transparency** is the trade-off: deep orthographies make derivational relationships visible (*sign* / *signature*, *bomb* / *bombard*, *muscle* / *muscular*) that shallow orthographies obscure when the underlying phonology has eroded.

The book benefits from this framework as a **closing analytical move**: each historical case study is also an answer to "where on the depth spectrum did this orthography end up, and why?" The frontier/interior/purge framework explains *why* reform succeeds or fails; the depth framework explains *what's actually at stake* — the literacy payoff. The two frameworks compose: one is policy, one is outcome. A final chapter pairing them gives the book a satisfying theoretical close after the historical sweep.

### Korean Hangul (1443): The Blank-Slate Case at State Scale

Mentioned in passing in the existing notes; deserves its own chapter paired with Faroese as the second blank-slate anchor:

- **King Sejong's *Hunminjeongeum*** (1443/1446) is the most extensively designed alphabet in history. **Featural design**: consonant letterforms iconically represent place and manner of articulation (ㄱ resembles the back of the tongue against the velum; ㄴ resembles the tongue against the alveolar ridge; ㅁ is a square representing closed lips). Vowels combine three primitives representing heaven (•), earth (—), and human (ㅣ). The phonological theory is embedded in the letter shapes themselves.
- **The class-politics of literacy.** Sejong's preface explicitly frames the project as making literacy accessible to commoners: "the common people, having no means of expressing their thoughts, suffer hardship." The aristocratic *yangban* class opposed Hangul precisely *because* it democratized literacy — Chinese characters were the prestige script and gatekeeper.
- **Four-century marginalization.** Hangul was suppressed (briefly) under King Yeonsangun in 1504, then marginalized for centuries as "women's script" and "vulgar script," then revived by nationalist scholars in the late 19th century, then suppressed under Japanese colonial rule, then fully institutionalized after 1945. The script that survived because the people kept using it informally for 400 years until political conditions allowed it to surface.

Faroese and Hangul together make the strongest paired blank-slate case study: small-scale community choice (Faroese 1846) vs. large-scale state design (Hangul 1443). Different conditions, very different time horizons, but both designed *against named alternatives*, both reflecting deliberate orthographic philosophy, both eventually triumphant against initial resistance. Hangul also introduces the **literacy-democratization** angle that complicates the "orthography as identity marker" reading — sometimes orthographic design is explicitly about expanding access to writing, with class politics as the central battleground.

### Chinese Simplified vs. Traditional: BCMS at Civilizational Scale

The BCMS analysis is the existing book's anchor case for orthography-as-identity-marker. A much larger-scale parallel exists in Chinese:

- **The 1956/1964 PRC simplification** reduced ~2,000 commonly-used characters from their traditional forms. Goals: improve literacy rates, ease pen-and-ink writing, reduce strokes per character. Achieved measurable gains in adult-literacy programs.
- **Taiwan and Hong Kong retained traditional characters.** Politically distancing from PRC orthographic reform; preserving continuity with pre-1949 print culture. Hong Kong's retention through the 1997 handover and into the 2020s is now itself politicized — simplified-character public signage is read as a political signal.
- **Singapore adopted simplified**, despite English-language administration. Linguistic alignment with mainland Chinese even where the political-administrative center is elsewhere.
- **The two systems are mutually intelligible** for educated readers, but reading speed drops 20–30% when encountering the unfamiliar system. Compare BCMS: the linguistic substrate is constant; the script is the political variable.

The Chinese case adds:
- A **scale** (1.3 billion users) where BCMS is small.
- A **success-condition pattern**: simplification succeeded because the PRC had absolute authority over education, publishing, and signage during a totalizing political moment. Reform requires either consensus (Italian, Finnish) or totalizing power (Turkish, Chinese simplified, Korean post-1945 Hangul-only).
- A **motte-and-bailey instance**: "PRC reformed Chinese for literacy" (motte, partly true) vs. "simplified is objectively better than traditional" (bailey, contested by Taiwanese scholars on grounds of etymological transparency and aesthetic continuity).

### Vietnamese Quốc Ngữ: The Colonial Imposition That Became a National Success

Vietnamese is the case study that **complicates the "indigenous reform good, imposed reform bad" intuition** the book might otherwise drift into:

- **Alexandre de Rhodes (1651).** Jesuit missionary in Vietnam developed the Latin-based Quốc Ngữ script for catechism work. The **diacritic system for Vietnamese's six tones** is elegant and became one of the most distinctive features of Vietnamese typography (the tone marks plus vowel-quality diacritics produce the densely-marked appearance of written Vietnamese).
- **French colonial mandate.** French administration mandated Quốc Ngữ for official Vietnamese-language documents in the late 19th century, displacing the older *Chữ Nôm* (Sino-Vietnamese characters) and *Chữ Hán* (classical Chinese).
- **Post-independence retention.** Both North and South Vietnam retained Quốc Ngữ after the French left. The Communist government, despite anti-colonial framing on every other dimension, kept the colonial-origin script because it was already serving the literacy program effectively. Adult literacy in Vietnam rose from ~10% in 1945 to ~95% by 1979, largely on the Quốc Ngữ base.

This case proves orthographic improvements **don't have to come from within**. The post-independence retention shows the test is uptake, not origin. Adds rigor to the typology by forcing the book to distinguish "reform that succeeded because it was indigenous" from "reform that succeeded because it improved literacy access, regardless of origin." The two factors can pull in opposite directions, and Vietnamese is the case where the second beat the first.

### English Reform Failures: The Unreformable System

The existing notes touch on English implicitly via the etymology-as-spelling-heuristic section. But **failed reform attempts in English** are themselves a major case study category, and "why English can't be reformed" deserves a chapter:

- **Noah Webster (1828).** *American Dictionary of the English Language*. Successfully shipped modest reforms: *color* (← *colour*), *center* (← *centre*), *theater* (← *theatre*), *defense* (← *defence*), *check* (← *cheque*), *jail* (← *gaol*). **Failed to ship** more aggressive reforms: *tung* (for *tongue*), *iland* (for *island*), *wimmen* (for *women*), *masheen* (for *machine*). The successful reforms were modest visual changes preserving morphology; the failures were aggressive phonetic respellings. Even within a single reformer's program, the boundary between accepted and rejected was tight.
- **The Spelling Reform Association (1876)** and **Simplified Spelling Board (1906, funded by Andrew Carnegie).** Lobbied for phonetic spelling reform. **Theodore Roosevelt issued an executive order** in 1906 requiring federal documents to use simplified spellings. The Supreme Court refused to comply. Congress repealed the order within months. The Carnegie funding wound down by 1915 with essentially nothing achieved.
- **George Bernard Shaw.** Famous for the *ghoti* joke (rough + women + nation = "fish") and for endowing a prize for a new English alphabet. Won by Ronald Kingsley Read's **Shavian alphabet** (1958). Has had essentially zero uptake despite being published in a Shaw-funded edition of *Androcles and the Lion*.
- **Why English reform has failed where others succeeded.** (1) No central language authority, no Académie analog. (2) Global distribution makes coordination impossible — even if the U.S. agreed, the U.K., Australia, India, Nigeria, and South Africa each have their own institutions. (3) Literary continuity from Shakespeare through dictionaries has high cultural stakes. (4) The etymological information in deep spellings serves morphological literacy (*sign / signature*, *bomb / bombard*). (5) Digital tooling (autocorrect, spell-check) has made the existing system tolerable enough that the literacy cost is increasingly shouldered by software rather than felt by individual writers.

English is the longest-running case of an unreformable major orthography in the modern world, and the persistent attempts to reform it form a rich micro-history. The chapter would naturally pair with the French 1990 *rectifications* case as "what happens when reform fails to gain political traction."

### Algorithmic Standardization: The Post-Institutional Reform Mechanism

A contemporary angle the existing notes don't address — and arguably the most important development in orthographic policy in the past 25 years:

- **Autocorrect as orthographic authority.** Predictive keyboards on iOS, Android, Gboard, and ChromeOS now standardize spelling for billions of writers without any institutional involvement. Each tech company's lexicon team makes orthographic choices that propagate at scale. *Cellphone* vs. *cell phone* vs. *cellular phone* — the predictive-text default wins. The Apple lexicon team in Cupertino has more day-to-day orthographic influence over English than any institution since Webster.
- **Search engines and orthographic gravity.** Google's "did you mean" function corrects spelling toward the most-searched variant. This creates a **feedback loop** where the spelling with marginal majority usage attracts further usage. Algorithmic reinforcement of standard forms beyond what any committee could achieve.
- **Emoji as a quasi-orthographic system.** A new pictographic layer added to all written languages simultaneously, with its own Unicode-Consortium-governed standardization. The first internationally adopted *writing system* since the Latin alphabet, in some senses.
- **Casual orthographic erosion of capitalization.** The capital-letter-as-marked-form is shifting; *i* for *I* is now common in casual texting and not stigmatized. Sentence-initial caps fade in chat. These changes are happening without anyone proposing them.
- **The hashtag and the @-mention** are new orthographic primitives that didn't exist 25 years ago. They function morphologically — *#* creates a marked-noun-phrase convention, *@* creates a vocative-direct-address marker — and their use is now standardized across platforms.

**Book implication**: orthographic reform now happens via *product*, not via committee. The Académie française can do nothing about Apple's autocorrect lexicon. The book is operating at a **transitional moment** where the institutional-reform story of the past 500 years gives way to an algorithmic-standardization story. A closing chapter or epilogue addressing this would be timely, would let the book speak to a contemporary policy conversation that the historical case studies otherwise can't, and would let the frontier/interior/purge framework be applied to a category of reform that didn't exist when the framework was first proposed.

### Smaller Topics Worth a Note

These don't merit chapters but earn flags in the running motifs:

- **The spelling bee as orthographic-depth artifact.** The Scripps National Spelling Bee, the cultural prominence of competitive English spelling — these exist *only* in Anglosphere countries because spelling competence is only a real achievement in deep orthographies. Finnish has no spelling bee culture. The Bee is a phenomenon that *implicitly diagnoses* what English asks of its readers. Possible opening anecdote for a chapter.
- **Punctuation reform as adjacent category.** The German 1996 reform changed comma rules in subordinate clauses. The Oxford comma is a live policy question. French guillemets vs. German guillemets vs. English curly quotes — same function, three conventions, identity-marker effect. The Spanish inverted opening punctuation (¿ ¡, adopted by the RAE in 1754) is the rare punctuation reform that stuck within its language community and zero adoption beyond. Worth at least a running mention.
- **Yiddish YIVO standardization (1936)** as the stateless-language case. YIVO's *Klal-shprakh* succeeded within secular Yiddish but never reached Orthodox Yiddish-speaking communities (who retained Hebrew-etymology-preserving spellings, a German-style "deep" approach). Demographic collapse via the Holocaust truncated what would have been a longer test of the standard. Adds: standardization can succeed without state authority within a defined community; demographic conditions determine ultimate reach; religious-secular splits often map onto orthographic-depth choices.
- **The reformer's personality profile.** Across successful cases, a recurring pattern: outsider-status combined with mainline credibility. Mikael Agricola (educated abroad, Lutheran outsider returning home); Atatürk (military outsider to Ottoman scholarship); King Sejong (sovereign personally invested); V.U. Hammershaimb (folklorist with Copenhagen academic standing working for his home community); Vuk Karadžić (folk-song collector with international scholarly recognition). Failed reformers often lack one dimension: Shaw had mainstream prestige but no institutional reach; Roosevelt had political authority but no orthographic-domain credibility; the French 1990 committee had institutional credibility but no popular reach. The reformer's personality as a small recurring biographical thread could give the book human texture beyond the structural arguments.

### Suggested Reading for the Nine-Year Runway

In addition to the languages already named (Greek, Danish, Finnish):

- **Florian Coulmas**, *The Writing Systems of the World* (1989) and *Writing Systems: An Introduction to Their Linguistic Analysis* (2003) — the most rigorous accessible survey. Includes the depth framework.
- **Amalia Gnanadesikan**, *The Writing Revolution* (2009) — popular but careful; good model for voice and pacing. Includes Hangul, Cherokee, and other designed scripts with care.
- **Geoffrey Sampson**, *Writing Systems* (1985, rev. 2015) — the typological classic. Argues for the depth framework explicitly.
- **Henry Rogers**, *Writing Systems: A Linguistic Approach* (2005) — pedagogical, comprehensive, less narrative.
- **John DeFrancis**, *Visible Speech: The Diverse Oneness of Writing Systems* (1989) — Chinese-focused but with broader theoretical reach; the locus classicus for the argument that all writing systems are ultimately sound-based.
- **David Crystal**, *Spell It Out* (2012) — popular English-focused. Useful model for what works at the popular end of the spectrum.
- **Seymour, Aro & Erskine (2003)**, "Foundation literacy acquisition in European orthographies" (*British Journal of Psychology* 94:143–174) — the empirical study underlying the depth-affects-reading-acquisition claim. Worth reading once for the citation.

For the Finnish dive specifically, Fred Karlsson's *Finnish: A Comprehensive Grammar* (Routledge) is the standard reference; *Finnish: An Essential Grammar* (also Routledge) is the lighter version. Both are solid for an adult learner without a teaching schedule.

### Hawaiian: Twelve Letters, the Allophone-Selection Problem, and the 1978 Restoration

Hawaiian is the cleanest case in the world for a category the existing typology doesn't yet have an anchor for: **what happens when an orthography must choose between phonological allophones in a language with substantial free variation**. It's also a successful language-revival case in the Hebrew / Catalan lineage, a missionary-designed orthography comparable to Vietnamese Quốc Ngữ but with very different historical conditions, and the minimum-grapheme-set example for the book's depth-vs-inventory discussion.

The mechanics:

- **1820s alphabet design.** American Congregational missionaries arriving in 1820 (Hiram Bingham and the *Thaddeus* mission) created the orthography for Bible-translation purposes. Early drafts included consonants the missionaries heard but couldn't reliably distinguish — b/p, d/k/t, l/r — and an 1826 consolidation settled on the now-standard 12 letters: 5 vowels (a, e, i, o, u) plus 7 consonants (h, k, l, m, n, p, w). The *ʻokina* (glottal stop) is treated as a thirteenth letter in modern Hawaiian orthography, not a diacritic. The *kahakō* (macron) marks long vowels as a diacritic. The result is among the smallest functional alphabets for any major language.
- **Continental vowel values: the "missionary vowel system."** The 1826 alphabet chose *i* for /i/ — the Latin/Romance convention, not the English one. English readers seeing *Maui* reach for /aɪ/ or /ɪ/ values; the Hawaiian /i/ is actually /i/ as in Latin, French, or Italian. **Pre-missionary English-language maps used English-style phonetic spellings**: Cook's journals (1778–79) and subsequent British/American mariners wrote *Owhyhee* for *Hawaiʻi*, *Mowee* for *Maui*, *Woahoo* for *Oahu*, *Atooi* for *Kauaʻi*, *Onehow* for *Niʻihau* — the "ee" digraph capturing /iː/ via natural English print convention. The Cook-era maps preserve in cartographic amber the orthographic road not taken. **The missionaries explicitly rejected this Anglo-phonetic tradition in favor of a Continental five-vowel system** — their instincts shaped by Yale and Andover Seminary Latin-Greek classical training rather than English vernacular print. The "i" choice is **suboptimal for English-reading visitors** (and produces the persistent mainland mispronunciations of *Maui*, *Kauaʻi*, *Hawaiʻi*) but optimal for Hawaiian-speaking readers, internationally aligned with Latin / Romance / IPA conventions, and more economical (single letter vs. digraph for a high-frequency vowel). The trade-off exposes the meta-question every orthographic decision answers in passing: **whose reading habits is this orthography for?**

  The same Continental-vowel convention appears in essentially every missionary-designed alphabet of the 18th and 19th centuries — Māori (Samuel Marsden et al., 1820s), Swahili (built on UMCA missionary precedents from the 1840s), Yoruba (Samuel Crowther and CMS, 1840s), Wolof, Guarani (Jesuits, 17th c.), Quechua (Spanish missions), and dozens more — to the point that linguistic taxonomy sometimes calls it **"the missionary vowel system."** A 19th-century Bible translator was much more likely to be a classicist than an Anglicist, and the Latin-vowel convention is the visible trace classical education left on the orthographies of dozens of unrelated languages. **The Hawaiian *i*-vs-*ee* decision sits inside this wider pattern.** A chapter could open with the Cook-era *Owhyhee* maps as a hook: that's what Hawaiian would have looked like if the colonial-British-Anglican mariner tradition had won the orthography-design contest. The American missionaries' continental choice is a small but real piece of **English-prestige pressure being explicitly resisted in the design of the alphabet** — by Americans who could have gone English-flavored and chose not to. The choice has cost English-reading tourists pronunciation accuracy for two centuries and counting; the missionaries explicitly priced that cost in.
- **The k/t allophone-selection problem.** The missionaries observed that Hawaiian had free variation between /k/ and /t/ depending on speaker, island, and context — and a parallel /l/ ↔ /n/ ↔ /d/ ↔ /r/ alternation. They picked one member of each cluster (k, l) as the orthographic standard and let the others fall away from writing. The choices were partly motivated by ease of teaching Latin-alphabet readers and partly by which variant predominated in the speech of the *aliʻi* (chiefs) the missionaries worked with on Hawaiʻi Island. **The Niʻihau dialect, on the privately-held island that never fully integrated into the modern Hawaiian-language standard, preserves /t/ to this day**: speakers there say *tapu* where standard Hawaiian writes *kapu*, *Tamehameha* where the standard writes *Kamehameha*. Niʻihau Hawaiian is the conservative variety the missionary alphabet didn't reach in time to normalize, and its survival makes Hawaiian the textbook case of *one allophone frozen by orthographic decision while the other survives dialectally as a geographical accident*.
- **The *w* question.** Hawaiian /w/ has a famously variable realization that the orthography doesn't capture. Standard descriptions:
  - After **front vowels** (/i/, /e/), realized as [v] or [β] — the same voiced bilabial fricative as Spanish intervocalic /b/ in *cabo*, *haba*, *árbol*. Your "cabo" observation is exactly right for this context.
  - After **back vowels** (/a/, /o/, /u/) and **initially**, realized as [w].
  - Many speakers vary even within a single word, and the variation tracks dialect, formality, and individual idiolect. *Hawaiʻi* is pronounced [haβaiʔi] by some speakers, [hawaiʔi] by others, with both accepted as standard. **The very name of the language and place sits on this allophonic boundary** — which is the kind of "the central example is itself the central problem" detail that makes for a strong chapter opening.
- **The *ʻokina*-as-letter decision.** The glottal stop is phonemic in Hawaiian — *kou* "your" vs. *koʻu* "mine", *kau* "to place" vs. *kaʻu* "mine", *ʻōlelo* "language" without the initial *ʻokina* is unparseable. The missionaries' eventual decision to treat *ʻokina* as a *letter* rather than a mere mark or diacritic is consequential: it gave the glottal stop full orthographic citizenship and made its omission feel as wrong as omitting any other consonant. Compare with Arabic *hamza* (treated as a diacritic-like mark sitting on a host letter — a weaker orthographic claim) or English (no orthographic representation at all for the glottal stops English does use, e.g., the medial stop in *uh-oh*). Hawaiian's choice was the stronger one and is foundational to how the orthography reads on the page.
- **The *Lānaʻi* / *lānai* case — the public-facing demonstration.** The single strongest illustration of why the *ʻokina* matters, and an immediate "wait, really?" moment for a general reader. The minimal pair:
  - ***Lānaʻi*** (with *kahakō* and *ʻokina*) is the island — three syllables, [laː.ˈna.ʔi], glottal stop before the final /i/.
  - ***lānai*** (with *kahakō* but no *ʻokina*) is the Hawaiian word for "porch, veranda" — two syllables, [laː.ˈnai̯], the final two vowels forming a diphthong.
  - ***lanai*** (English loan with both marks stripped) is what appears in real-estate listings and resort signage across Hawaiʻi — and is *also* what *Lānaʻi* (the island) collapses to in the same diacritic-stripping print conventions, producing genuine surface-form ambiguity between island and architectural feature.
  
  The pair is pedagogically richer than the usual textbook minimal pair because **three distinctions collapse at once** when the marks are dropped: word identity (island vs. veranda), syllable count (3 vs. 2), and stress / vowel pattern. Most minimal-pair demonstrations show one contrast; *Lānaʻi* / *lānai* shows a stack of three collapsing under a single typographic decision. It's also a *daily, contemporary* problem in Hawaiian English — every condo brochure uses *lanai* (veranda), every island reference uses *Lanai* (the island), and you can't tell from print alone which is meant without context. The contemporary-stakes texture lets the chapter open with a hook that lands in 2026, not 1826.

  The pair also exemplifies a **general principle worth naming**: stripping diacritics under prestige-language pressure produces ambiguity *exactly where the original orthography was doing genuine disambiguating work*. French *café* → English *cafe* doesn't matter because no English ambiguity arises. Spanish *piñata* → English *pinata* loses phonetic information but no semantic information. But Hawaiian *Lānaʻi* → English *Lanai* and Hawaiian *lānai* → English *lanai* both collapse to the same surface form — and the original marks were doing *semantic* disambiguation, not just phonetic decoration. **The cost of stripping diacritics is calibrated by how much disambiguating work those marks were doing in the donor language.** This principle generalizes beyond Hawaiian — German *Maße* / *Masse* under ß-to-ss simplification, Vietnamese tone-mark dropping in URLs and SMS, French homograph collisions when the circumflex is stripped — and could anchor a recurring analytical thread throughout the book.
- **The 20th-century neglect.** Through the territorial period (1898–1959) and statehood (1959–), Hawaiian-language use was actively suppressed in schools. English-language typesetting in Hawaiʻi-related publications routinely dropped the *ʻokina* and *kahakō*, partly from typewriter-keyboard limitations, partly from English-prestige pressure. Place names appeared in stripped-down form: *Hawaii* without the *ʻokina*, *Oahu* without the *kahakō*, *Kalakaua* without the second *ʻokina*. By 1980, native speakers numbered in the low thousands, mostly elderly — and the orthographic stripping mirrored a population-level near-extinction of the language.
- **The 1978 constitutional restoration.** The Hawaiʻi state constitutional convention amended the state constitution to make Hawaiian a co-official language alongside English. This created institutional pressure to standardize and respect Hawaiian orthography in state documents, signage, and education materials — gradually undoing the twentieth-century print convention of stripping marks. The *ʻokina* and *kahakō* slowly returned to official Hawaiian-language usage through the 1980s and '90s. **Pūnana Leo** ("language nest"), the Hawaiian-immersion preschool movement launched in 1983, made consistent orthography pedagogically essential when teaching Hawaiian as L1 to children of non-native-speaker parents. Native-speaker numbers grew through immersion education for the first time in a century.

The book-fit angles, all of them productive:

1. **Allophone selection as orthographic decision** (new category). The k/t case is the cleanest demonstration in the book of orthography forced to choose between free-variant allophones, and the Niʻihau-preserves-/t/ angle gives it a *geographical-political accident* texture missing from the existing case studies. The category fills a real gap in the typology — none of the current case studies anchor "what does an alphabet do when the language gives it equally-good options?" Hawaiian provides the cleanest answer: it picks one and lets the dialect carry the other.
2. **Missionary-designed orthography**. Pairs directly with Vietnamese Quốc Ngữ as a non-colonial-state missionary creation. Adds: where Vietnamese Quốc Ngữ was carried by French colonial state authority, Hawaiian was carried by religious authority and persisted partly *as a religious-community alternative to colonial English-only*. The two cases together let the book distinguish missionary-imposed-by-state-power from missionary-imposed-by-religious-community-cohesion.
3. **The minimum-grapheme-set case.** Twelve letters plus the *ʻokina* is among the smallest functional alphabets for any major language. Useful for the "how few letters do you actually need?" question — *and* useful for arguing that orthographic depth and grapheme inventory are *independent dimensions*. Hawaiian is shallow-but-tiny; Finnish is shallow-but-medium-sized; English is deep-but-medium-sized; Chinese is deep-and-massive. The two axes vary independently.
4. **Language-revival as orthographic-reform driver.** The 1978 restoration is a successful reform tied to political-cultural revitalization, paralleling Hebrew, Catalan, Welsh, and Māori. The Hawaiian-Hebrew comparison is particularly clean because both languages were nearly extinct as community languages by mid-20th century, both required orthographic decisions as part of revival, and both revived via institutional commitment to early-childhood immersion (Pūnana Leo in Hawaiian, Ulpanim in Hebrew). The Māori *Kura Kaupapa* movement (1985) and the Welsh *Mudiad Meithrin* (1971) follow the same pedagogical pattern; Hawaiian is the U.S. anchor for that comparative thread.
5. **Restoration as a fourth regime in the closing synthesis.** The frontier/interior/purge framework currently has three regimes. The Hawaiian 1978 case — restoration of orthographic marks that had been dropped under English-prestige pressure — doesn't fit any of them cleanly. It's not protecting the frontier; it's not contesting the interior; it's not purging foreign loans. It's *restoring previously-standard conventions that had eroded*. Adding **restoration** as a fourth regime would let the framework cover this case explicitly. Welsh-language road-sign restoration in Wales, post-1990s Maori-orthography re-officialization in New Zealand, and recent Catalan-orthography reaffirmation in Catalonia all fit this fourth regime.
6. **The *w*-as-[β] observation as opening hook.** The fact that Hawaiian *w* sounds nothing like English *w* to English ears — and instead sounds like the Spanish intervocalic *b* in *cabo*, a sound English speakers don't typically attend to as a phoneme — is the kind of "wait, really?" moment that opens a chapter well. Reader is immediately curious what else the orthography is doing that doesn't match expectations.

Reading recommendations for the Hawaiian dive:

- **Mary Kawena Pukui and Samuel H. Elbert**, *Hawaiian Dictionary* (1986, revised). The lexicographic standard, with substantive front-matter notes on phonology and dialectal variation, including the *w* realization question and the k/t merger.
- **Albert J. Schütz**, *The Voices of Eden: A History of Hawaiian Language Studies* (1994). The standard history of the missionary-alphabet design process; covers the 1820s decisions in detail and is honest about which choices were principled and which were expedient. Probably the single most useful book if you wanted to write the Hawaiian chapter well.
- **William H. Wilson and Kauanoe Kamanā**, founding-figure writings on the Pūnana Leo and Ka Haka ʻUla O Keʻelikōlani College of Hawaiian Language at UH Hilo. The revival-movement perspective on why orthographic consistency became pedagogically critical when teaching Hawaiian as L1.
- **Larry Kimura's** scholarly writings on Hawaiian dialectal variation, especially the Niʻihau preservation of /t/. Kimura is the central figure in the academic linguistic study of Niʻihau Hawaiian.

The chapter would naturally sit alongside Hebrew (revival), Vietnamese (missionary design), and the BCMS / Chinese cases (orthography and identity), and would let the closing synthesis introduce **restoration** as a fourth regime alongside frontier, interior, and purge.
