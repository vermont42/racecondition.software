# How One Latin Verb Became "Success," "Problem," and "Death"

*A possible first language video. The seed: while bringing Spanish frequency data
into Conjugar, the data surfaced obscure verbs the way it once surfaced French
`issir` for Conjuguer. That pulled a thread that runs from one Latin verb out to
four modern languages — and each language gave it a different emotional color.*

## The core idea

The big concept the video teaches is **semantic drift**: words keep their shape
across centuries but quietly change meaning, and because each language drifts
*independently*, a single ancestral word ends up meaning different — sometimes
opposite — things in its descendants. Semantic drift is one of the main engines by
which human languages diverge over time. You can *see* the family resemblance in the
spelling while the meanings have wandered off in different directions. That visible
gap between form and meaning is what makes it good video material.

The hook: take **one** Latin verb and show that it became the Spanish word for
*success*, the English word for *a problem*, and the French euphemism for *death* —
all the same word underneath.

## The etymon

Latin **exīre** = *ex-* ("out") + *īre* ("to go") → "to go out." Its supine /
noun form is **exitus** = "a going-out, an outcome." Everything below descends from
this one verb-and-noun pair.

(Nice aside: the *īre* element is the same one Spanish `ir` and the English word
*exit* carry. So the audience already owns a piece of the root before the video
starts — `exit` literally is *exitus*.)

## The cognate fan-out (the spine of the video)

The same word, "the way things turned out," drifted into four different emotional
registers:

- **Spanish `éxito`** → **success**. Specialized *positive*: "the way it turned out"
  came to mean "the way it turned out **well**."
- **English `issue`** → **problem / point in dispute** (also offspring, edition,
  outcome). Drifted toward the *negative/contested* — "we have an issue."
- **French `issue`** (fem. noun) → **exit / outcome**, neutral, but its most fixed
  phrase is grim: *issue fatale* = a fatal outcome, the euphemism for death.
- **Italian `esito`** → **outcome**, flatly *neutral*: *esito positivo / negativo*.

Italian is the punchline that proves the point: `esito` is the formal cognate of
`éxito` but kept the neutral meaning. That makes **Spanish the outlier** — the one
language that decided "the outcome" should mean "the *good* outcome." English is the
other outlier in the opposite direction. The drift wasn't random noise; you can show
two languages pulling the same word in opposite directions.

## The `issir` / `issu` sub-thread (the "lost verb" beat)

French once had the *verb* **`issir`** ("to go out"), from `exīre`. The verb died,
but its past participle **`issu`** survives in everyday French: *issu de* = "descended
from / arising from" (*issu d'une famille modeste*, *issu de l'immigration*). A verb
that lives on only through one inflected form is a vivid image — a fossil still in
use.

- English borrowed the French feminine noun **`issue`** directly — that's where our
  word comes from.
- The "descended from" sense of `issu` is exactly archaic English **"died without
  issue"** (without offspring). Same idea, two languages.

Spanish had the parallel verb too: **Old Spanish `exir`** ("to go out"), all over the
*Cantar de Mio Cid*, later killed off by `salir` (which is unrelated — from *salīre*,
"to jump"). So Spanish and French *both* inherited `exīre`, both abandoned the verb,
but only French kept the participial fossil. Catalan/Valencian **`eixir`** is the
sister language where the verb never died at all — still everyday speech for "to go
out." A great way to show that "dead" is language-specific.

## Why this connects to my apps

Honest origin story for the intro: I build verb-conjugation apps (Conjugar for
Spanish, Conjuguer for French). Frequency-ranked word lists, fed into the apps,
keep surfacing verbs I'd never met — French `issir` was one; Spanish `adir` (alive
almost only in the legal phrase *adir la herencia*) was another. Defunct verbs that
survive through a single frozen form are a recurring character. That's a natural,
non-preachy on-ramp from "I make these apps" to "here's a thing the data taught me."

   with Italian as the neutral control that proves drift is directional.
4. The lost verb: issir → issu, Old Spanish exir → salir, Catalan eixir still alive;
   "without issue."
5. Close on the big idea: this is semantic drift, and it's *how languages diverge* —
   shared inheritance, independent wandering. Tease the next one (other Latin verbs
   that split this way — there are many).
