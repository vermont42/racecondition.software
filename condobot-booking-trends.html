An analysis of the full booking history of our four vacation rentals — two condos in Kailua‑Kona, the Sands of Kahana condo on Maui, and the Skyline Drive house above Lake Tahoe. The question: when does demand land, how far ahead do guests book, who actually stays, and what should we do about it? Numbers come straight from Hospitable's reservation history; the charts and the “so what” are the point.

Read this first. The four properties have wildly different histories on the booking platforms, so their sample sizes aren't comparable and I never blend them into one number. Kanaloa went live in March 2026 — it has only a few months of data, so treat every Kanaloa figure as a placeholder, not a trend. Banyan (since fall 2024) is suggestive; Maui and Tahoe (4–5 years on-platform) are solid. Everything here is realized history — stays that have already happened — so it can't see the already-booked future, which matters most for far-ahead Maui.

The portfolio at a glance

Revenue is host revenue (net of channel fees) over each property's time on the platform — not comparable across properties because the windows differ, but it tells you where the money is. n = realized stays analyzed.

1. How far ahead guests book — the biggest difference between properties

This turned out to be the richest signal in the whole dataset, bigger than day of week or even season. Where a guest books on this timeline dictates how we should price and what cancellation policy makes sense. Each bar splits a property's bookings by how many days before arrival they were made — warm (left) is last‑minute, cool (right) is far ahead.

  • Maui books ~9 months out (median 265 days; 56% of stays booked 240+ days ahead). That's a loyal, plan-far-ahead, repeat-guest crowd.
  • Tahoe is two crowds at once — a big last‑minute cluster (ski/lake weekenders) and a far‑ahead cluster (holiday and prime-summer planners).
  • Banyan sits in the middle at ~4 months.
  • Kanaloa's 14‑day median is an illusion — a listing born in March can't have long lead times yet. Ignore it.
So what: Don't drop Maui's prices to fill a gap three weeks out — that's simply not when its guests shop; instead open Maui's calendar 12–18 months ahead so the far-out planners can book. Tahoe is the property that rewards last‑minute discounting, because it actually has last‑minute demand. And because Maui bookings sit on the calendar for the better part of a year, a firmer cancellation policy or larger deposit protects it far more than it would Tahoe. If anything the 265‑day Maui figure understates how far ahead its guests really plan: the longest‑lead bookings — placed now for 2027 stays — haven't arrived yet, so they aren't in this data. Opening the calendar early is the safe call, not a gamble.

2. Are bookings spiky, or a steady drip? (Amanda's question)

Amanda asked two good questions: do bookings spike around certain dates or events — weekends and the like, or is it a consistent stream? And does booking volume react to outside events, e.g. people booking less when oil or airfare prices jump? Here's what the data can and can't say.

Day of the week a booking is placed — no weekend spike

If guests booked mostly on weekends (browsing on a lazy Sunday) you'd see it here. They don't. Bookings arrive in a remarkably even stream all week — if anything a touch lower on weekends than mid‑week.

All four properties pooled, n=499 bookings. Bars are the share of bookings placed on each weekday; the dashed line is a perfectly even 1/7 (14.3%).

As for “a consistent amount of time out” — that's the lead‑time story above, and the answer is it depends on the property: Maui is consistent (almost everyone books far ahead), while Tahoe is the opposite of consistent — two separate crowds, one last‑minute and one far‑ahead.

Booking volume over time — and the honest limit on the “events” question

This is every booking in the history, counted by the month it was placed, with the airline‑fare price index drawn over it (right axis). The recurring January bumps are New‑Year trip planning (mostly Tahoe); the big step up in fall 2024 is simply Banyan coming online, and the small tick in spring 2026 is Kanaloa. Eyeball the fare line against the bars: the bars' big moves line up with listings and January, not with the fare line.

All four properties pooled (bars, left axis) with BLS airline‑fare CPI overlaid (line, right axis). Markers note when listings were added — those, not guest behavior, drive the biggest moves. The greyed recent months are incomplete: a booking placed lately for a far‑off stay hasn't arrived yet, so it isn't in the data — those months are excluded from the test below.

So I did the careful version Amanda asked for: take the booking line, divide out the growth that's purely more listings (bookings per live unit), subtract each month's normal seasonality (which also soaks up the January surge), and ask whether airfare or oil prices explain any of what's left — at the booking month and up to three months earlier. Below, each dot is one month with all that structure removed: left‑right is how far airfare sat above or below normal, up‑down is how our bookings sat above or below normal. A real “pricey flights → fewer bookings” effect would tilt the cloud down to the right.

52 months (Feb 2021–May 2025), seasonality removed from both axes. The dashed line is the best fit — nearly flat. It's a shapeless blob, not a downhill trend.
The honest verdict: inconclusive — and here's exactly why. After removing listings and seasonality, there is no trustworthy link between fuel/airfare prices and our booking volume. The one result that even looks statistically real points the wrong way (it says higher fares go with more bookings) — and it vanishes the moment you account for the fact that fares and our bookings simply drifted upward together after COVID. That's a classic coincidence‑of‑trends, not cause and effect. Crude oil shows nothing at all.

Why we can't be sure either way: there are only ~50 usable months, most of the early ones with just a property or two; and — frustratingly — the only big fare move on record (airfares jumped ~25% over winter–spring 2026) lands in exactly the recent stretch we had to grey out, because those bookings haven't arrived in the data yet. So the cleanest natural experiment is the one window the data won't let us use. A blank result here means “not enough data,” not “prices don't matter.”

So what: don't price or market around fuel and airfare on the strength of this — there's no reliable signal. The single highest‑value re‑check is to run this again in 9–12 months, once that 2026 fare spike has matured into usable bookings. (Full method, regression table, and caveats: docs/booking-trends.md, Appendix A.)

3. When guests come — every property has its own season

There is no single “peak season” across the portfolio. Bars show occupied nights per year in each month (how full the place is); the line shows check‑ins per year (how many separate bookings arrive). Both are averaged per observed year — corrected for how long each listing has actually been live — so the month-to-month shape is honest. Watch how differently the four behave.

  • Tahoe has a true double peak. Winter (Feb–Mar) brings the most check-ins — lots of short ski weekends. Summer (Jun–Aug) brings the most nights — fewer, longer lake stays. The clear soft patch is the Sep–Nov shoulder (plus a dip in May).
  • Hawaii is nearly flat year‑round. Maui and Kona fill up about the same every month, with only a gentle late‑summer (Aug–Sep) dip — so for Hawaii the seasonal lever is the nightly rate (next chart), not occupancy.
So what: Tahoe deserves an aggressive, season‑aware calendar — premium rates and longer minimum stays over the Dec holidays, Feb–Mar ski weeks, and Jun–Aug lake season, with discounts and relaxed minimums in the Sep–Nov shoulder where the empty nights actually are. Hawaii doesn't need heavy seasonal rules; lean on the winter rate premium and prop up the Aug–Sep soft spot with a targeted promotion.

4. The winter rate premium

Even where occupancy is flat, what guests pay per night isn't. This plots each property's average nightly rate by month against its own annual average (100 = that property's typical night), so the three very different price points (Maui runs nearly double the others) can share one chart. Above the line = a premium month.

So what: December–January (holidays, plus whale season in Hawaii) and Tahoe's February ski peak carry a real premium every year — hold the line on rates then. The troughs (Hawaii's early fall, Tahoe's October and May) are where a modest discount or a min‑stay relaxation does the most good.

5. Who actually stays — and what to buy

This is the question with the most concrete payoff. The share of stays that bring children varies a lot by property, and it tells us how to furnish and describe each one. (Pets are essentially zero everywhere — consistent with the no‑pets rules — so they're not worth a cent of furnishing.)

  • Maui and Tahoe are family properties — the two biggest units (both sleep 8), and roughly 4 in 10 stays include a child. Maui in summer is family-heavy specifically: 55% of June–August stays bring kids.
  • Banyan is a couples / small‑group property — median party of 4, and only 1 in 5 stays brings a child. Its whole framing (oceanfront corner, two kings, romantic lanai) matches who books it.
So what — the shopping list:
  1. Tahoe: buy a pack‑n‑play and a high chair. The listing currently tells guests no crib is provided — yet 42% of stays bring kids. Clearest gap in the portfolio.
  2. Maui: add a high chair and pack‑n‑play and time a toy/games refresh for early summer, when most guests bring children. Lean the summer listing copy family‑friendly.
  3. Banyan: leave it couples‑framed. Don't dilute the oceanfront-romance angle with kid‑gear messaging 80% of its guests don't need.

6. Which platform, and how often they cancel

The two long‑established properties live on Vrbo; the two new Kona units are Airbnb‑first — which lines up neatly with the lead‑time story (Vrbo skews to an older, plan‑ahead crowd, exactly Maui's profile).

Separately, cancellations run a steady ~21–25% of already‑arrived bookings on the three established properties (Kanaloa's 5% is just too new to read). That's about one in four or five — easy to forget when you're eyeballing a full-looking calendar, and it bites hardest on Maui, where the highest cancellation rate meets the longest lead times.

Method & caveats (for the curious)
  • Source: every reservation Hospitable has for each property from 2020‑01‑01 through 2026‑06‑14, keeping only accepted guest stays (cancellations, declined inquiries, and owner blocks removed). Cancellations are counted separately, never mixed in.
  • Realized history only. The data ends today, so it contains no future-dated reservations — the right basis for “what has demand looked like,” but it can't see the booked-but-not-yet-arrived pipeline (largest for far-ahead Maui).
  • Seasonality is normalized per observed year. Because the data ends in mid-June, the first half of the year would otherwise be over-counted; each month is divided by the number of years it was actually observable. “Live” is dated from each listing's first booking (not first arrival), since a listing takes bookings before its first guest arrives. June 2026 is itself a half‑month at the cutoff, so June is mildly under‑counted — don't over‑read one month's exact height.
  • Sample sizes: Tahoe 207, Maui 163, Banyan 110, Kanaloa 19 realized stays. Kanaloa and (to a lesser degree) Banyan are provisional. Percentiles (medians, etc.) on small samples are nearest‑rank, so an n=19 or per‑month figure can wobble.
  • Money is host revenue net of channel fees, a reasonable owner-economics proxy, not a full profit‑and‑loss. Nightly rate is an unweighted per‑stay average (a 2‑night and a 14‑night stay count equally); the “rate premium” chart shows each property indexed to its own average.
  • Booking weekday/month are bucketed in UTC, so a booking placed late on a Pacific‑time Sunday can land on Monday — immaterial to patterns this size, but it means the weekday chart is approximate.
