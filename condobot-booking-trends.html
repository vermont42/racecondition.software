An analysis of the full booking history of our four vacation rentals — two condos in Kailua‑Kona, the Sands of Kahana condo on Maui, and the Skyline Drive house above Lake Tahoe. The question: when does demand land, how far ahead do guests book, who actually stays, and what should we do about it? Numbers come straight from Hospitable's reservation history; the charts and the “so what” are the point.

Read this first. The four properties have wildly different histories on the booking platforms, so their sample sizes aren't comparable and I never blend them into one number. Kanaloa went live in March 2026 — it has only a few months of data, so treat every Kanaloa figure as a placeholder, not a trend. Banyan (since fall 2024) is suggestive; Maui and Tahoe (4–5 years on-platform) are solid. Everything here is realized history — stays that have already happened — so it can't see the already-booked future, which matters most for far-ahead Maui.

The portfolio at a glance

Revenue is host revenue (net of channel fees) over each property's time on the platform — not comparable across properties because the windows differ, but it tells you where the money is. n = realized stays analyzed.

1. How far ahead guests book — the biggest difference between properties

This turned out to be the richest signal in the whole dataset, bigger than day of week or even season. Where a guest books on this timeline dictates how we should price and what cancellation policy makes sense. Each bar splits a property's bookings by how many days before arrival they were made — warm (left) is last‑minute, cool (right) is far ahead.

  • Maui books ~9 months out (median 265 days; 56% of stays booked 240+ days ahead). That's a loyal, plan-far-ahead, repeat-guest crowd.
  • Tahoe is two crowds at once — a big last‑minute cluster (ski/lake weekenders) and a far‑ahead cluster (holiday and prime-summer planners).
  • Banyan sits in the middle at ~4 months.
  • Kanaloa's 14‑day median is an illusion — a listing born in March can't have long lead times yet. Ignore it.
So what: Don't drop Maui's prices to fill a gap three weeks out — that's simply not when its guests shop; instead open Maui's calendar 12–18 months ahead so the far-out planners can book. Tahoe is the property that rewards last‑minute discounting, because it actually has last‑minute demand. And because Maui bookings sit on the calendar for the better part of a year, a firmer cancellation policy or larger deposit protects it far more than it would Tahoe.

2. When guests come — every property has its own season

There is no single “peak season” across the portfolio. Bars show occupied nights per year in each month (how full the place is); the line shows check‑ins per year (how many separate bookings arrive). Both are averaged per observed year, which corrects a subtle bias toward the first half of the year. Watch how differently the four behave.

  • Tahoe has a true double peak. Winter (Feb–Mar) brings the most check-ins — lots of short ski weekends. Summer (Jun–Aug) brings the most nights — fewer, longer lake stays. The clear soft patch is the Sep–Nov shoulder (plus a dip in May).
  • Hawaii is nearly flat year‑round. Maui and Kona fill up about the same every month, with only a gentle late‑summer (Aug–Sep) dip — so for Hawaii the seasonal lever is the nightly rate (next chart), not occupancy.
So what: Tahoe deserves an aggressive, season‑aware calendar — premium rates and longer minimum stays over the Dec holidays, Feb–Mar ski weeks, and Jun–Aug lake season, with discounts and relaxed minimums in the Sep–Nov shoulder where the empty nights actually are. Hawaii doesn't need heavy seasonal rules; lean on the winter rate premium and prop up the Aug–Sep soft spot with a targeted promotion.

3. The winter rate premium

Even where occupancy is flat, what guests pay per night isn't. This plots each property's average nightly rate by month against its own annual average (100 = that property's typical night), so the three very different price points (Maui runs nearly double the others) can share one chart. Above the line = a premium month.

So what: December–January (holidays, plus whale season in Hawaii) and Tahoe's February ski peak carry a real premium every year — hold the line on rates then. The troughs (Hawaii's early fall, Tahoe's October and May) are where a modest discount or a min‑stay relaxation does the most good.

4. Who actually stays — and what to buy

This is the question with the most concrete payoff. The share of stays that bring children varies a lot by property, and it tells us how to furnish and describe each one. (Pets are essentially zero everywhere — consistent with the no‑pets rules — so they're not worth a cent of furnishing.)

  • Maui and Tahoe are family properties — the two biggest units (both sleep 8), and roughly 4 in 10 stays include a child. Maui in summer is family-heavy specifically: 55% of June–August stays bring kids.
  • Banyan is a couples / small‑group property — median party of 4, and only 1 in 5 stays brings a child. Its whole framing (oceanfront corner, two kings, romantic lanai) matches who books it.
So what — the shopping list:
  1. Tahoe: buy a pack‑n‑play and a high chair. The listing currently tells guests no crib is provided — yet 42% of stays bring kids. Clearest gap in the portfolio.
  2. Maui: add a high chair and pack‑n‑play and time a toy/games refresh for early summer, when most guests bring children. Lean the summer listing copy family‑friendly.
  3. Banyan: leave it couples‑framed. Don't dilute the oceanfront-romance angle with kid‑gear messaging 80% of its guests don't need.

5. Which platform, and how often they cancel

The two long‑established properties live on Vrbo; the two new Kona units are Airbnb‑first — which lines up neatly with the lead‑time story (Vrbo skews to an older, plan‑ahead crowd, exactly Maui's profile).

Cancellations run a steady ~21–25% of guest bookings on the three established properties (Kanaloa's 5% is just too new to read). That's about one in four or five — easy to forget when you're eyeballing a full-looking calendar, and it bites hardest on Maui, where the highest cancellation rate meets the longest lead times.

Method & caveats (for the curious)
  • Source: every reservation Hospitable has for each property from 2020‑01‑01 through 2026‑06‑14, keeping only accepted guest stays (cancellations, declined inquiries, and owner blocks removed). Cancellations are counted separately, never mixed in.
  • Realized history only. The data ends today, so it contains no future-dated reservations — the right basis for “what has demand looked like,” but it can't see the booked-but-not-yet-arrived pipeline (largest for far-ahead Maui).
  • Seasonality is normalized per observed year. Because the data ends in mid-June, the first half of the year would otherwise be over-counted; each month is divided by the number of years it was actually observable.
  • Sample sizes: Tahoe 207, Maui 163, Banyan 110, Kanaloa 19 realized stays. Kanaloa and (to a lesser degree) Banyan are provisional.
  • Money is host revenue net of channel fees, a reasonable owner-economics proxy, not a full profit‑and‑loss.
  • Built by CondoBot's analysis script; the underlying writeup lives in the project repo. Generated 2026‑06‑14.
